Muscle-building advice is full of rules: stay in the 8–12 rep "hypertrophy range", never do cardio, train every muscle twice a week, always go to failure. Over the last decade, large meta-analyses have tested most of these, and many have not survived.

Here is the short version:

- **Volume is the main lever.** More hard sets per muscle per week means more growth, with diminishing returns.
- **Load barely matters for size**, as long as sets are taken close to failure. It matters a lot for strength.
- **Effort matters.** Growth improves as sets end closer to failure, but going all the way to failure adds little.
- **Frequency matters little for size** once weekly volume is equal. It does help strength.
- **Stretching is a weak growth stimulus** unless done for very long periods, and long static stretches right before lifting can reduce growth.
- **Isometrics do build muscle**, especially when held at long muscle lengths, but strength gains stay specific to the position trained.
- **Strength + hypertrophy** combine easily: heavy work for strength, moderate-load volume for size.
- **Power is the fragile one.** High-fatigue training and same-session cardio both blunt explosive gains.
- **Cardio + hypertrophy** is far more compatible than gym folklore suggests, especially if you separate the sessions and favour cycling over lots of running.
- **Cardio inside the lifting session** works: circuits and supersets build muscle about as well as traditional sets while adding some aerobic fitness, as long as each muscle still gets enough rest. They improve VO2max less than dedicated cardio.

The rest of this post goes through the studies behind each point.

## How I picked the studies

I leaned on, in rough order of trust:

1. **Large meta-analyses and meta-regressions**, ideally pre-registered and with direct measures of muscle size (ultrasound, MRI, biopsies) rather than body-weight scales.
2. **Well-controlled randomised trials** where they answer a specific question, such as velocity-loss training or within-subject cardio designs.
3. **Recent preprints** only where they add a large, pre-registered test, and labelled as such.

Most of this research involves young, untrained or recreationally trained adults over 6–12 weeks. Keep that in mind; I return to it at the end.

## Part 1: The core variables for muscle growth

### Volume: the strongest dose-response

The first major meta-analysis on weekly set volume pooled 34 treatment groups from 15 studies and found a graded dose-response: more weekly sets per muscle produced more growth[^1]. When volume was split into categories (fewer than 5, 5–9 and 10+ weekly sets), the trend favoured 10+ sets but fell just short of statistical significance.

A follow-up trial in resistance-trained men tested 1, 3 and 5 sets per exercise, three times a week, for 8 weeks. That works out to roughly 6–9, 18–27 and 30–45 weekly sets per muscle. Muscle growth kept climbing with volume, but strength gains were similar across all three groups[^2]. That split between size and strength recurs throughout this post.

The most comprehensive analysis so far, by Pelland and colleagues at Florida Atlantic University, ran multi-level meta-regressions across 67 studies[^3]. Volume increased both hypertrophy and strength, but strength hit diminishing returns much sooner. The authors also introduced a "fractional" set count, where an indirect set (a bench press set for the triceps, say) counts as half a set. That method predicted outcomes best, which is a useful way to count your own volume.

The largest network meta-analysis of resistance training prescriptions, with 119 randomised trials and 3,364 participants for hypertrophy, agrees: every program built muscle compared with doing nothing, and the top-ranked programs for hypertrophy were the ones using multiple sets[^4].

### Load: heavy or light, size is similar

Pedro Lopez and colleagues analysed 28 studies with 747 adults, including only trials where sets went to volitional failure[^5]. Low loads (more than 15 reps), moderate loads (9–15 reps) and high loads (8 reps or fewer) produced similar muscle growth. Strength was a different story: heavy and moderate loads clearly beat light loads for 1RM gains.

Currier's network meta-analysis found the same pattern: loads above 80% of 1RM maximised strength, while all prescriptions produced comparable hypertrophy[^4].

So the "hypertrophy rep range" is a convenience, not a requirement. Moderate reps let you accumulate a lot of hard sets without the joint stress of very heavy loads or the misery of 30-rep sets.

### Proximity to failure: close, but not necessarily all the way

A 2023 meta-analysis of 15 studies found only a trivial advantage for failure over non-failure training, and no advantage when studies used the strict definition of momentary muscular failure[^6].

A 2024 series of meta-regressions across 55 studies estimated reps in reserve (RIR) for each group and treated it as a continuous variable[^7]. Hypertrophy improved as sets ended closer to failure. Strength gains, by contrast, were similar across a wide range of RIR; load mattered more for strength. The authors flag that the RIR estimates were indirect, so the exact curve is uncertain.

Practically: for hypertrophy work, most sets should end around 0–3 reps short of failure. For strength work, stopping well short of failure is fine.

### Frequency: helps strength, not much size

In the Pelland meta-regressions, once weekly volume was accounted for, training a muscle more often added little or nothing measurable to growth. Strength, however, kept improving with frequency, with diminishing returns[^3]. Frequency is mainly a tool for spreading volume so each session stays productive.

### Range of motion: the long-muscle-length question

A 2023 meta-analysis found a trivial overall edge for full range of motion over partials. A sub-analysis hinted that partial reps performed at long muscle lengths ("lengthened partials") might grow muscle slightly more than full range, but the confidence interval crossed zero[^8].

A large pre-registered cluster trial across 15 sites (posted as a preprint) then compared full range of motion with lengthened partials in trained lifters over 12 weeks and found broadly similar changes in muscle size and strength[^9].

My read: emphasising the stretched part of an exercise is reasonable, but it is a small effect at best. Full range of motion remains a safe default.

### Stretching: a weak growth stimulus, and a possible drag before lifting

Stretching gets linked to muscle growth in three different ways: stretching on its own, stretching between sets, and stretching before a session. The evidence differs for each.

**Stretching on its own can grow muscle, but only at doses few people would do.** Two 2024 meta-analyses reach slightly different conclusions:

- Warneke and colleagues pooled 42 studies with 1,318 participants. They found a small but significant effect of static stretching on muscle size (effect size 0.20)[^38]. Longer stretching sessions, longer programs and more frequent stretching produced significant effects, while lower doses did not. The authors described stretching as being of minor effectiveness compared with resistance training.
- Arntz and colleagues pooled 25 studies and found a trivial overall effect (0.12) with a prediction interval crossing zero, which they called unclear[^39]. More total time under stretch was linked to more growth, and gains in flexibility were not related to gains in muscle size.

The most striking trial put 69 active people into three groups for 6 weeks[^40]. One group stretched the calf for **one hour a day, seven days a week**, using an orthosis. Another did 5 sets of 10–12 calf presses three times a week, taking about 15 minutes per session. Both groups gained similar strength and similar thickness in the inner calf muscle (medial gastrocnemius), though only the lifting group grew the outer calf muscle significantly. As the authors point out, the stretching group needed up to 7 hours a week to match about 45 minutes of lifting. That makes stretching an interesting option for rehabilitation, when lifting isn't possible, but not a practical way to build muscle.

**Stretching between sets: mixed results, probably small.**

- In untrained people, 30 seconds of static stretching between sets for 8 weeks produced a trend toward more growth. The authors said the data were not sufficient to conclude it was better than normal training[^41].
- In a within-subject calf study of 21 untrained men, a 20-second loaded stretch between sets may have increased growth in the soleus (the deeper calf muscle), but the effect on the gastrocnemius was uncertain[^42].
- In 26 trained men doing bench press for 8 weeks, a 30-second loaded stretch between sets (on cable flyes, at 15% of the working load) made no difference to chest thickness or strength, positive or negative[^43].

**Stretching right before lifting can cost you growth.** In a 10-week trial, each participant had one leg assigned to stretching first. That leg did two 25-second quadriceps stretches immediately before leg extensions, and the other leg went straight into lifting. The stretched leg completed fewer reps and less total volume, and its muscle grew less (7.4% vs 12.7% increase in cross-sectional area), even though strength gains were the same[^44]. This fits the volume findings above: anything that cuts the work you can do in a set can cut growth.

**What this means in practice:**

- Don't count on stretching to build muscle. The muscle-growth benefit of the stretched position is better gained by lifting through long muscle lengths (see range of motion above).
- Avoid long static stretches of the muscle you are about to train. Save them for after the session or another time of day.
- Stretching between sets is optional. It appears harmless in trained lifters and may help a little, but the evidence is thin.
- If flexibility is your goal, stretching works for that. Full range of motion lifting also improved flexibility in the calf trial above[^40].

### Isometrics: they build muscle, especially at long muscle lengths

Isometric training means contracting a muscle without the joint moving: holding a position, or pushing against something that doesn't move. It can grow muscle, and the same long-muscle-length theme from the range of motion section shows up again.

**Joint angle matters most.** A systematic review of 26 isometric training studies found that holding at **long muscle lengths** produced more muscle growth (about 0.9–1.7% per week) than the same volume at short muscle lengths (about 0.1–0.8% per week)[^45]. Muscle growth and strength gains occurred across a wide range of contraction intensities. High-intensity contractions (70% of maximum or more) were needed to improve tendon properties. Training with ballistic intent (contracting as fast and hard as possible) improved rapid force production more. Long-length isometrics also carried over better to dynamic performance.

A good example is a trial in which 16 men did isometric knee extensions three times a week for 6 weeks[^46]. Those holding with the knee bent (long quadriceps length) grew their quadriceps by about 5%, while those holding with the knee nearly straight (short length) did not grow significantly[^47]. The long-length group also improved their concentric (moving) strength across a range of speeds more.

**Even flexing with no load can grow muscle, in beginners.** In a within-subject study, 13 untrained people trained one arm by contracting their biceps as hard as possible through a full range of motion with no weight, and the other arm with curls at 70% of 1RM. Over 18 sessions, both arms grew similarly, but the weighted arm gained more strength (about 2.3 kg vs 1 kg on the 1RM)[^48]. This shows that hard contractions, not the weight itself, are the stimulus. But it is a single small study of untrained people.

**Flexing between sets: a small, mixed effect.** In 27 trained men over 8 weeks, adding 30 seconds of hard no-load flexing of the working muscle at the start of each rest period produced more growth in the mid-thigh but no difference in the other muscles measured. It may also have slightly reduced leg press strength gains[^49].

**Strength is specific to how you train.** A 2025 meta-analysis of 32 studies found isometric training improved isometric strength more than dynamic training did, but the two produced similar gains when strength was tested through movement on a machine that controls speed (isokinetic testing)[^50]. If your goal includes lifting heavier weights through a full range of motion, isometrics alone are not enough.

**The gap.** I could not find a meta-analysis that directly compares isometric and dynamic training for muscle growth, only individual trials and a registered trial that has not reported yet. So "isometrics grow muscle about as well as lifting" is plausible but not well established.

**What this means in practice:**

- Isometrics are a legitimate tool when equipment is limited, a joint is painful through part of a movement, or you are rehabilitating an injury.
- Hold in the lengthened position (bottom of a split squat, deep in a calf raise, a stretched-position chest or hamstring hold), and contract hard.
- Use heavy isometrics (70% of maximum effort or more) if tendon health is a goal, and explosive-intent isometrics if rapid force matters for your sport.
- Keep dynamic lifting as the main driver if you want to get stronger at actual lifts.

### Nutrition: protein and energy balance

The landmark protein meta-analysis pooled 49 randomised trials with 1,863 participants[^10]. Protein supplementation modestly increased gains in fat-free mass and strength, and the benefit plateaued at a total intake of about 1.6 g per kg of body weight per day.

Energy matters too. A meta-analysis of resistance training in an energy deficit found that lean mass gains were impaired compared with training at energy balance, and a deficit of roughly 500 kcal per day was enough to prevent lean mass gains on average[^11]. Strength still improved in a deficit, at least in the mostly novice participants studied. This is very relevant to hybrid training: lots of cardio on top of lifting makes it easy to end up in an unintended deficit.

## Part 2: Hybrid approaches

### Hypertrophy + strength

This is the easiest pairing, because the two goals share most of their inputs.

**Volume-equated, different loads.** In a classic trial, trained men did either a bodybuilding-style routine (3 sets of 10RM, 90 seconds rest) or a powerlifting-style routine (7 sets of 3RM, 3 minutes rest) for 8 weeks, with total volume load matched[^12]. Muscle growth was similar, but the heavy group gained more bench press strength. The catch is time: the heavy routine took far longer per session to match the work.

**Strength is load- and frequency-sensitive; size is volume-sensitive.** Across the meta-analyses above, heavy loads drive 1RM gains[^5][^4], frequency helps strength more than size[^3], and extra volume keeps paying for size long after it stops paying much for strength[^2][^3]. Proximity to failure matters for size, but much less for strength[^7].

**Periodisation helps strength, not size.** A meta-analysis of 35 volume-equated studies found periodised programs produced slightly larger 1RM gains than non-periodised ones (effect size 0.31), and undulating periodisation beat linear in trained people. Muscle growth did not differ between any of the approaches[^13].

**What this looks like in practice:**

- Start sessions with one heavy compound lift: 3–6 reps, stopping 1–3 reps short of failure.
- Follow with moderate-load hypertrophy work: 6–15 reps, ending 0–2 reps short of failure.
- Count weekly hard sets per muscle (half credit for indirect sets) and progress volume over a training block.
- Train each main lift 2–3 times a week if strength is a priority, since frequency helps strength.

### Hypertrophy + power

This is the trickiest pairing. Power depends on fast, high-quality contractions, and the fatigue that helps hypertrophy can work against it.

**Velocity loss: a direct trade-off.** Pareja-Blanco and colleagues had 22 young men squat for 8 weeks, stopping each set when bar speed fell by either 20% or 40%[^14]. Strength gains were similar. The 20% group improved jump height far more (9.5% vs 3.5%) despite doing 40% fewer reps. The 40% group grew more muscle in two of the quadriceps muscles, but lost some of its fastest (type IIX) muscle fibres, while the 20% group kept them. In other words, training closer to failure bought size at the cost of explosiveness.

**Plyometrics can build muscle too.** A review of eight studies found plyometric training and resistance training produced similar whole-muscle growth in the legs, at least over 12 weeks or less in untrained and recreationally trained people[^15]. Combining the two did not seem to add growth beyond resistance training alone, but that comparison rests on very few studies.

**Cardio hits power hardest.** The largest concurrent-training meta-analysis found that adding aerobic training did not compromise hypertrophy or maximal strength, but it did blunt explosive strength (effect size −0.28), mainly when both were done in the same session rather than at least 3 hours apart[^16]. A 2026 umbrella review of 17 meta-analyses confirmed the pattern: hybrid training matched resistance training for strength and size but produced smaller power gains[^17].

**What this looks like in practice:**

- Do power work first in a session, when you are fresh: jumps, throws, Olympic lift variations or fast-intent lifts.
- Keep power sets short and crisp. Stop when speed drops noticeably, well before failure.
- Put the high-fatigue hypertrophy work after the power work or on separate days.
- Keep hard cardio away from power sessions, ideally on different days or at least several hours apart.

### Hypertrophy + cardio

The "interference effect" was first described in the 1980s, and the fear that cardio "kills gains" has persisted ever since. The evidence has softened considerably.

**The older view (Wilson et al., 2012).** This meta-analysis of 21 studies found hypertrophy was smaller with concurrent training than with lifting alone (effect sizes 0.85 vs 1.23)[^18]. Running caused interference with hypertrophy and strength, but cycling did not. More frequent and longer cardio sessions were associated with worse strength, power and hypertrophy outcomes.

**The updated view (Schumann et al., 2022).** With 43 studies and around 1,090 participants, this analysis found essentially no effect of added aerobic training on hypertrophy (effect size −0.01) or maximal strength[^16]. The results held regardless of cardio type, training frequency, training status or age. Only explosive strength suffered, as covered above.

**The fibre-level caveat (Lundberg et al., 2022).** Looking at biopsies rather than whole muscles, 15 studies showed a small negative effect of concurrent training on muscle fibre growth (effect size −0.23)[^19]. The effect was clearest for type I fibres when the cardio was running (−0.81), but not cycling. So running may still carry a cost that whole-muscle measurements miss.

**HIIT.** A meta-analysis of 13 studies found concurrent HIIT and resistance training produced similar muscle growth and upper-body strength to resistance training alone, with a small reduction in lower-body strength gains[^20].

**Can cardio even help?** In a within-subject design, ten men did 5 weeks of knee-extension training on both legs, with one leg also doing one-legged cycling. The cycling leg grew about twice as much (6% vs 3%) even with only 15 minutes between cycling and lifting[^22]; an earlier version with 6 hours between sessions also found greater growth in the concurrent leg[^21]. These studies are tiny, used single-joint training and partly untrained lifters, and some of the extra size may have been fluid rather than contractile tissue. They do show the interference effect is not inevitable.

**Order and spacing.** The 2026 umbrella review suggests lifting before cardio may slightly favour strength and size when both are done in one session[^17]. A 2026 review of sequencing studies found no consistent effect of order on long-term hypertrophy in human trials[^23]. Separating sessions by at least 3 hours mainly protects explosive strength[^16].

**What this looks like in practice:**

- Most people can do 2–3 cardio sessions a week without measurable loss of muscle growth.
- Prefer cycling, rowing or other low-impact options if you are doing a lot of cardio, especially near leg days.
- If both happen on one day, lift first or split them by several hours.
- Eat enough. Extra cardio raises energy needs, and a deficit, not the cardio itself, is the more likely reason for stalled gains[^11].
- Watch total running volume. The strongest signals of interference come from high-frequency, long-duration running[^18][^19].

### Hypertrophy and cardio in the same session

Instead of adding separate cardio, many people try to get both from the lifting itself: circuits, supersets, short rest, high reps, kettlebells, alternating sides on single-limb exercises. The question is whether this builds muscle as well as normal training while also raising aerobic fitness. The short answer: it can build muscle just as well if it is set up carefully, and it does improve aerobic fitness, but less than dedicated cardio.

**Normal lifting barely moves VO2max in fit people.** A review of traditional resistance training studies found that only 3 of 17 studies in young adults showed a significant increase in VO2max[^24]. Gains appeared mainly in people starting with low aerobic fitness (below about 40 mL/kg/min in young adults), and rep range, from 3RM to 28RM, made no difference. In a meta-analysis of 66 trials in adults with overweight or obesity, resistance training improved VO2max less than aerobic training[^25]. So simply doing high reps does not turn lifting into cardio. The rest periods and session structure matter more than the rep count.

**Circuit training: the strongest evidence for "both at once".** In circuits, you move from exercise to exercise with little rest, so your heart rate stays high throughout.

- A 2017 meta-analysis found resistance circuit training improved both VO2max and bench press strength compared with controls. The largest VO2max gains came from programs of about 14–30 sessions over 6–12 weeks, with sessions of at least 20–30 minutes at 60–90% of 1RM[^26].
- A larger 2021 meta-analysis of 45 studies found circuits increased muscle mass by about 1.9% and VO2max by about 6.3%, and improved upper- and lower-body strength[^27].
- The key trial for hypertrophy-focused readers is Alcaraz and colleagues (2011). Trained men did either heavy circuits (6RM loads, about 35 seconds between exercises) or traditional strength training for 8 weeks. Strength and lean mass gains were similar, and the circuit sessions were shorter[^28]. An earlier acute study showed the trick: rotating through bench press, leg extensions and calf raises, each muscle still got several minutes of rest, so bar speed and reps matched traditional sets while the cardiovascular load was much higher[^29].

**Supersets: same muscle, less time.** A 2025 meta-analysis of 19 studies found supersets produced similar long-term gains in muscle size, strength and strength endurance to traditional sets, with shorter sessions, higher blood lactate and harder-feeling workouts[^30]. The type of pairing mattered. Agonist–antagonist supersets (say, rows with presses) let people complete more reps, while supersets hitting the same muscle twice reduced the total weight lifted.

**Short rest: the main thing to avoid for the same muscle.** A 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis found a small hypertrophy advantage for resting more than 60 seconds between sets, probably because short rest cuts how much weight you can lift on later sets. Beyond about 90 seconds, longer rest made no detectable difference[^31]. That is why the circuit and superset findings work: total session rest is short, but each individual muscle still recovers.

**Cluster sets: a power tool, not a cardio tool.** Cluster sets split a set into mini-sets with short breaks (for example, 2 reps, 15 seconds rest, repeated). A meta-analysis of 29 studies found no difference from traditional sets in strength, power or hypertrophy[^32]. Another meta-analysis of 17 studies found that cluster and rest-redistribution sets improved speed and power at submaximal loads slightly more than traditional sets, while traditional sets were better for muscular endurance[^33]. Cluster sets are designed to reduce fatigue, which makes them a good fit for the power hybrid above and a poor fit for conditioning.

**Single-limb and asymmetrical exercises.** A 2025 meta-analysis of 9 studies found no difference in muscle growth between unilateral (one limb at a time) and bilateral training. Strength gains were specific: unilateral training improved single-limb strength more, and bilateral training improved two-limb strength more[^34]. Unilateral work fits well into dense sessions, because each side rests while the other works, which naturally creates a superset. I found no good trials testing whether offset or asymmetrical loading (a suitcase carry, a single-arm press) improves aerobic fitness or muscle growth more than symmetrical loading, so any claims there are speculation.

**Kettlebells and functional circuits.** In a small trial of 17 female college soccer players, four weeks of kettlebell snatch intervals improved VO2max by about 6%, while a circuit weight training group did not improve[^35]. A meta-analysis of whole-body HIIT (bodyweight circuits such as squats, push-ups and burpees) found it increased fat-free mass compared with no exercise and improved musculoskeletal fitness more than conventional cardio, but improved aerobic fitness slightly less (effect size −0.40)[^36]. A meta-analysis of high-intensity functional training found it matched both moderate cardio and conventional HIIT for endurance, but found no studies comparing it with dedicated resistance training for strength or size[^37].

**The overall pattern.** Lifting in a dense format can build muscle at about the same rate as normal lifting while adding a moderate aerobic stimulus. But the aerobic gains are smaller than dedicated cardio produces, and are largest in people who start out unfit. If VO2max is a real goal, combining dense lifting with some dedicated interval or steady cardio is still the best-supported option.

**What this looks like in practice:**

- Pair exercises so the same muscle is not worked back to back: upper with lower, push with pull, or left side with right side.
- Keep 30–60 seconds between exercises, so each muscle still gets roughly 2–3 minutes before its next set.
- Choose loads from about 6 to 30 reps per set. Lighter loads build as much muscle as heavier ones if sets go close to failure, but less strength[^5]. Heavier loads (roughly 60–90% of 1RM) were linked to the largest VO2max gains in circuit studies[^26].
- With higher reps, check you are actually near failure. In a dense session, breathing may end a long set before the muscle is close to its limit. That is my reasoning, not a tested finding.
- Save very short rest and the hardest pace for a finisher at the end: kettlebell intervals, a bodyweight circuit or a short bike sprint block of 8–12 minutes.
- Keep the heaviest strength work and the power work in a traditional format with full rest, using cluster sets if you want to stay fast.

## How much weight can this evidence bear?

- **Short studies.** Most trials last 6–12 weeks. We know little about how these trade-offs play out over years.
- **Who gets studied.** Mostly young, untrained or recreationally trained men. Highly trained lifters and elite hybrid athletes are rare in the data.
- **Measurement.** Ultrasound and MRI measure size, which can include swelling. Muscle damage after a session can inflate early "growth".
- **Modest cardio doses.** Few concurrent trials match the volumes of serious endurance athletes, so results for marathon-plus-bodybuilding plans are extrapolation.
- **Circuit studies vary widely.** "Circuit training" covers everything from light machine circuits to 6RM barbell rotations, and few trials measure muscle size directly rather than lean mass.
- **Estimated variables.** Reps in reserve and fractional sets were estimated across studies, so the exact curves are uncertain even though the direction is clear.

## Summary

| Goal | Main levers | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypertrophy | Weekly hard sets per muscle; sets ending 0–3 RIR; ~1.6 g/kg protein; no large deficit | Stalling in an energy deficit |
| + Strength | Add heavy compound work (>80% 1RM), 2–3x/week per lift; periodise | Heavy-only training is slow per set for building size |
| + Power | Power work first and fresh; low velocity loss; plyometrics also build leg muscle | Training to failure and same-session cardio blunt explosiveness |
| + Cardio | Low-impact modes; lift first or separate sessions; eat enough | High-volume running; unplanned calorie deficit |
| + Cardio in the same session | Circuits and antagonist or upper/lower supersets; 30–60 s between exercises; a short finisher | Resting under ~60 s between sets of the *same* muscle; expecting dedicated-cardio VO2max gains |

The big picture is reassuring. Muscle growth is robust: it tolerates a wide range of loads, frequencies and added cardio, as long as you do enough hard sets and eat enough. Strength asks for heavier loads and more practice. Power is the quality that most needs protecting in a hybrid program.
