One minute of hard sprinting, split into ten 6-second bursts, twice a week. In small trials, that was enough to raise aerobic fitness by 8% in untrained middle-aged adults and to lower blood pressure in people in their sixties.

Most interval training research uses 30-second all-out sprints, and most people hate them. Six-second sprints are a different experience. They are short enough to finish before the burn really sets in, which makes them easier to tolerate for older adults and beginners.

This post walks through the studies that used 6-second intervals: what they did, what changed, and how much weight the results can bear.

## Two kinds of 6-second interval

Not every 6-second study asks people to go flat out, and the difference matters when you read the results.

- **All-out sprints.** You pedal or run as hard as you possibly can for 6 seconds. Most of the studies below, including the Abertay (Scotland) and Macau research groups, used this approach.
- **Controlled supramaximal intervals.** The resistance is set in watts, above the power you produce at your maximal aerobic capacity but below your true maximum. The Umeå group in Sweden designed this version for older adults. Training started at 60% of each person's estimated maximal 6-second power and progressed from there.

Rest periods vary even more than effort. Studies used anything from 9 seconds to 2 minutes between sprints, and as the next section shows, that choice changes what the workout trains.

## What happens inside the muscle

A string of 6-second sprints starts out as anaerobic work and becomes increasingly aerobic as the sprints add up.

**Gaitanos et al. (1993).**[^1] Eight men did ten 6-second cycle sprints with 30 seconds of rest, with muscle biopsies before and after the first and tenth sprints. In sprint 1, phosphocreatine (the muscle's fast-acting fuel store) and glycolysis (breaking down sugar without oxygen) supplied the energy roughly equally, and phosphocreatine fell by 57%. By sprint 10, muscle lactate no longer rose at all, meaning glycolysis had almost stopped. Yet power output was still 73% of the first sprint. The authors attributed this to phosphocreatine and aerobic metabolism carrying the later sprints.

**Shi et al. (2018).**[^2] Thirteen male endurance runners did 40 × 6-second all-out cycle sprints with 15, 30 or 60 seconds of passive rest, and a work-matched Wingate session of 8 × 30-second sprints. Time spent at or above 90% of VO2max was 368 seconds with 15-second rests, 118 seconds with 30-second rests and 20 seconds with 60-second rests, against 74 seconds in the Wingate session. Shorter rests lowered the power of each sprint but raised the aerobic load.

The practical lesson: 6-second sprints are only easy on the heart and lungs when the rests are long. With short rests, they can keep you near maximal oxygen uptake for longer than a classic 30-second Wingate session does.

## Athletes and active people

In short training blocks, 6-second sprints improved endurance performance about as much as 30-second sprints, and the exact rest period mattered less than expected.

**Triathletes (Jakeman, Adamson & Babraj, 2012).**[^3] Six sub-elite triathletes did 10 × 6-second cycle sprints with 1 minute of rest, three times a week for 2 weeks: 6 minutes of sprinting in total. Compared with six controls who kept up their normal training, they improved aerobic performance and built up blood lactate more slowly.

**6 seconds vs 30 seconds (Lloyd Jones, Morris & Jakeman, 2017).**[^4] Thirty active men were split into a 6-second group (20 × 6 s, 48 s rest), a 30-second group (4 × 30 s, matched for total sprint time and the same 1:8 work-to-rest ratio) and a control group. After six sessions over 2 weeks, 10 km cycling time-trial performance improved by 5.1% in the 6-second group and 6.2% in the 30-second group, with no significant difference between them. Peak power rose more in the 30-second group (20% vs 9% across the training sessions). With about ten people per group, "no significant difference" does not prove the two protocols are equivalent.

**Rest periods (Lloyd Jones, Morris & Jakeman, 2019).**[^5] Thirty-six men and women did 10 × 6-second sprints with 48, 60 or 72 seconds of rest (work-to-rest ratios of 1:8, 1:10 and 1:12) for 2 weeks. All three groups gained 51–57 W of peak power and improved their time-trial performance, with no significant differences between them.

**Rugby players in hypoxia (Galvin et al., 2013).**[^6] Thirty academy rugby players did 12 sessions over 4 weeks of 10 × 6-second running sprints with 30 seconds of rest on a non-motorised treadmill, breathing either normal air or low-oxygen air (13% oxygen). Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Level 1 performance improved by 14% in normal air and 33% in low oxygen.

**Youth soccer players (Li & Xue, 2024).**[^7] Thirty-six players added 4 sets of 6–10 × 6-second all-out runs to their normal training for 7 weeks, at work-to-rest ratios of 1:3, 1:6 or 1:9. All three groups improved soccer performance, fitness and hormonal markers. Longer rests (1:9) gave larger gains in jumping, peak power and hormonal markers. Shorter rests (1:3) gave larger gains in aerobic capacity and the Yo-Yo test.

**Female team-sport athletes (Rogers, Gill & Beaven, 2024).**[^8] In a single session, 13 rugby sevens and netball players did 10 × 6-second cycle sprints with 60, 90 or 120 seconds of rest. Sixty-second rests lowered average power (710 W vs 734 W and 743 W) and made the session feel harder. As a one-session study, it suggests rather than proves that 90 seconds or more is better when power is the goal.

## Middle-aged and older adults

Once or twice a week of 6-second sprints improved fitness, everyday physical function and some cardiometabolic markers in middle-aged and older adults, in small trials from a handful of research groups.

**Middle-aged adults (Adamson et al., 2014a).**[^9] Fourteen untrained adults (mean age 43) were split into a control group and a training group of eight. The training group did 10 × 6-second cycle sprints with 1 minute of rest, twice a week for 8 weeks. VO2peak rose by 8%, physical function tests improved by 11–27%, and the blood glucose response to an oral glucose tolerance test fell by 6%.

**Older adults, first look (Adamson et al., 2014b).**[^10] This short research letter reported on groups of about six people aged around 65 who did 6–10 × 6-second cycle sprints twice a week for 6 weeks. Physical function and self-reported health both improved.

**Blood pressure (Adamson et al., 2019).**[^11] Seventeen adults aged 60–71 were assigned to a control group or 10 weeks of twice-weekly 6-second sprint training, with sessions lasting about 12 minutes by the end. Compared with controls, the training group lowered pulse pressure (56 to 49 mmHg), mean arterial pressure (102 to 93 mmHg) and the total-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (4.4 to 3.2), and got faster on the timed get-up-and-go test (7.4 to 6.6 s). Pulse pressure is an indirect marker of arterial stiffness; stiffness itself was not measured.

**Once or twice a week (Adamson et al., 2020).**[^12] Thirty-four adults (mean age 65) were assigned to no training, or 8 weeks of 6-second sprint sessions once or twice a week. Both training groups improved blood glucose control, physical function and aerobic capacity compared with the control group. Differences between once and twice a week were small to moderate for blood glucose and physical function, and trivial for aerobic capacity. The authors concluded that even one session a week was enough to produce health benefits.

**The Umeå HIT Study (Simonsson et al., 2023; Frykholm et al., 2024).**[^13][^14] Sixty-eight adults aged 66–79 who did not exercise regularly were randomised to 12 weeks of twice-weekly cycling. One group did controlled supramaximal intervals: 10 × 6 seconds with 54 seconds of recovery, in a 20-minute session. The other did moderate-intensity training: 3 × 8-minute intervals in a 40-minute session. Both groups improved cardiorespiratory fitness by a similar amount, even though the interval sessions took half the time. Effects on muscle function and everyday physical function were also similar, and the interval group gained more anaerobic cycling performance. A later analysis found improved heart rate variability in the interval group compared with the moderate-intensity group.[^15]

**Taking it out of the lab (Pettersson et al., 2025).**[^16] Four instructors at a training facility ran the Umeå programme for 21 older adults aged 65–78. The study concluded the programme can work in a community setting with support, such as individualised watt-based intensity and structured progression. Instructors found coordinating tasks during the intervals challenging and needed confidence in running the programme.

**Pedalling efficiency (Jabbour et al., 2017).**[^17] Seventeen young adults (mean age 26) and 13 middle-aged adults (mean age 55) did six 6-second supramaximal cycle sprints with 2 minutes of passive rest, three times a week for 6 weeks. Both groups improved performance, and the authors attributed this to better mechanical efficiency, meaning more work done for the same energy cost.

None of these trials was large enough to establish safety. Participants were screened before taking part, so the fairest summary is that the training was well tolerated by healthy, screened adults.

## Body fat and metabolic health

Two 12-week trials from the same Macau group found that high-repetition 6-second sprints matched longer interval training for fitness and weight loss, in much less exercise time.

**Visceral fat (Tong et al., 2018).**[^18] Forty-six obese young women did 12 weeks of 3–4 sessions a week, or no training. The sprint group did 80 × 6-second all-out cycle sprints with 9 seconds of rest, which is 8 minutes of sprinting per session. The comparison group did repeated 4-minute bouts at 90% of VO2max with 3 minutes of rest, until they had done 400 kJ of work. The authors concluded that the sprint protocol reduced abdominal visceral fat with a smaller training load and time commitment than the longer intervals.

**Fitness and insulin (Sun et al., 2019).**[^19] Forty-two overweight young women were randomised to sprint training (80 × 6 s with 9 s rest), high-intensity interval training of equal energy cost, or about 61 minutes of moderate cycling, three days a week for 12 weeks. All three groups improved VO2peak by about 25% and lost about 5% of body mass. Insulin sensitivity improved in the sprint and interval groups but not in the moderate group. Fasting glucose, however, fell only in the moderate group.

## Sidebar: six seconds as a test

Researchers also use a single 6-second sprint as a standard challenge to see how the body responds. Derbré et al. (2010) gave adolescent boys a 6-second cycle sprint test before and after 6 months of sprint training.[^20] After training, total testosterone rose more in response to the sprint. Bioavailable testosterone, the fraction the body can readily use, did not change. This was a study of sprint training in general, not of 6-second interval training, and the training group had only six boys.

## Sidebar: does creatine help?

Probably, at least for sprint performance, but no study has yet combined creatine with a 6-second sprint training program.

**Why it might work.** A 6-second sprint runs largely on phosphocreatine, and creatine supplements raise total muscle creatine by about 20%. More fuel in the tank, and possibly faster refilling between sprints, is exactly what repeated short sprints need.

**Within a single session, cycling results are encouraging.** After 5 days of 20 g a day, 14 active men did more total work and reached higher peak power across 80 minutes of repeated 6-second bike sprints with 24–84 seconds of rest.[^21] In another study, creatine raised peak and mean power over 10 × 6-second cycle sprints with either 30 seconds or 6 minutes of rest.[^22]

**Running results are weaker.** Creatine did not improve repeated 6-second treadmill sprints in college football and soccer players,[^23] or six 60 m sprints.[^24] One likely reason is that creatine adds 1–2 kg of body weight, mostly water, which matters when you carry it but not on a bike.

**Training adaptations are barely studied.** The closest evidence uses longer intervals. With 4 weeks of interval training, creatine improved the ventilatory threshold more than placebo (16% vs 10%) but added nothing to VO2peak.[^25] In a small 6-week study of 30-second Wingate training, the creatine group gained more leg strength and anaerobic power.[^26]

**Older adults.** In older adults doing resistance training, creatine added extra gains in fat-free mass, leg press strength and the 30-second chair stand test.[^27] Combining it with the Umeå or Abertay sprint protocols is an interesting idea that has not been tested.

The usual approaches are a 5-day loading phase of about 20 g a day, or simply 3–5 g a day, which reaches similar muscle levels in about 3–4 weeks. Creatine is well tolerated by healthy people, but anyone with kidney disease or on several medications should check with their doctor first.

## How much weight can this evidence bear?

The results are consistent and promising, but they come from small, short studies, so treat them as a strong hint rather than settled fact.

- **Small samples.** Most training groups had 6 to 16 people.
- **Short durations.** Most interventions lasted 2 to 12 weeks, so we know little about what happens over a year.
- **Few research groups.** Much of the work comes from three or four labs, mostly using cycle ergometers.
- **Likely overestimation.** A meta-analysis of 55 sprint interval training studies found a medium overall benefit for physical performance. It also found 41 very large effect sizes and evidence of small-study bias, and its authors warned that reported effects are probably inflated.[^28]

## What this means for you

The rest period is the main dial: short rests turn 6-second sprints into aerobic training, long rests keep them about power. The table below is a practical reading of the studies above, not a set of tested prescriptions.

| Goal | Example setup | Based on | Who it suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aerobic fitness in very little time | 40–80 sprints, 6 s on, 9–15 s rest | Shi 2018; Tong 2018; Sun 2019 | Healthy adults short on time |
| Endurance performance | 10–20 sprints, 6 s on, 30–72 s rest | Jakeman 2012; Lloyd Jones 2017, 2019; Galvin 2013 | Endurance and team-sport athletes |
| Health and function with age | 6–10 sprints, 6 s on, 54–60 s rest, once or twice a week | Adamson 2014–2020; Umeå HIT Study | Middle-aged and older adults, after medical clearance |
| Power and speed | 6–10 sprints, 6 s on, at least about 1 min rest; longer (up to 2 min) to keep each sprint near maximal | Li & Xue 2024 (1:9 ratio, 54 s); Rogers 2024 (single session) | Speed and power athletes |

Why rest longer for power than for health? A 6-second sprint runs mostly on phosphocreatine, which is only about 70% refilled after 30 seconds and takes up to about 3 minutes to recover fully. For power, each sprint needs to be near your best, so fuller recovery helps. For health, the goal is the total load on the heart, lungs and metabolism, so partial recovery is fine. The 1-minute rest in the health studies was a practical choice and was never compared with other rest lengths.

If you are older, have a heart condition or have not exercised in a while, talk to your doctor first. The controlled, watt-based approach from Umeå may be a gentler starting point than all-out sprints.
