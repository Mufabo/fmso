The people who win memory championships are not unusually intelligent, and their brains don't look structurally different from anyone else's. When researchers put ten of them in a brain scanner, they scored about the same as matched controls on tests of general cognitive ability. What set them apart was a strategy: nine of the ten said they used a technique invented roughly 2,500 years ago, and their brains lit up in regions used for spatial navigation while they memorised.[^1]

That technique is the method of loci, also called the memory palace. This post covers what it is, how well it works, what it does to the brain, and the question that matters most if you're thinking of practising it: does getting better at it make you better at anything else?

Along the way, we'll build a memory palace out of the opening three minutes of the Dark Souls Remastered glitchless world record.

## What mnemonics are

A mnemonic is any technique that makes information easier to remember by attaching it to something your memory already handles well. Some common ones:

- **First-letter mnemonics and acronyms.** "Every Good Boy Deserves Fudge" for the lines of the treble clef.
- **Rhymes and songs.** "Thirty days hath September."
- **The keyword method.** For vocabulary: link a foreign word to a similar-sounding word in your language, then picture the two meanings interacting.
- **Peg systems.** Memorise a fixed list of "pegs" (one-bun, two-shoe, three-tree) and hang new items on them.
- **The major system.** Convert digits into consonant sounds, then into words, so numbers become images.
- **The method of loci.** Place vivid images of the items along a route you know well, then walk the route in your mind to retrieve them.

They share a principle. Most things we need to remember, such as lists, numbers and names, are abstract and arbitrary. Human memory is much better at images, places and stories. Mnemonics translate the first kind of information into the second.

## How the method of loci works

The method has three ingredients:

1. **A route.** A sequence of distinct locations you can walk through in your head in a fixed order: rooms in your home, landmarks on your commute.
2. **Images.** Each item to be remembered becomes a vivid, preferably absurd or animated, picture.
3. **Placement.** You put one image at each location, ideally interacting with it. To recall, you walk the route and "look" at each spot.

The route does two jobs. It provides cues, because each location prompts the image you left there. And it preserves order, because you always walk the same way. That's why the method is especially good for ordered lists, such as a speech, a deck of cards or the steps of a procedure.

Why does it work so well? One explanation, from Martin Dresler, whose lab has studied memory athletes for years, is that it borrows brain systems that were vital for our ancestors. Knowing where things are and how to get back to them mattered for survival; remembering a list of unrelated words did not. The method of loci routes new information through the machinery for visual and spatial memory, which is very good at its job.

## Building a memory palace from a Dark Souls speedrun

The traditional advice is to use a place you know intimately, such as your childhood home. But your route doesn't have to be a physical place. In one study, 142 students explored a simple virtual building on a computer for just five minutes and then used it as their memory palace. They recalled about as well as students using a familiar real-world route, and both groups beat uninstructed controls by about 10–16%.[^2]

If a five-minute virtual walk works, a game world you've spent hundreds of hours in should work at least as well. Dark Souls is close to ideal for three reasons:

- **It's big.** The world is one large, interconnected map, from the Undead Asylum down to the Kiln of the First Flame. That's room for many palaces: each area can hold its own list, and the shortcuts between them give you natural ways to chain lists together.
- **It's detailed.** Every area is hand-built and visually distinct. Bonfires, boss arenas, doors, ladders, NPCs and item corpses give you hundreds of landmarks that are easy to tell apart, which matters because similar-looking loci are a common reason images get muddled.
- **It's deterministic.** Nothing is procedurally generated. Every enemy, item and corridor is in the same place on every playthrough, for every player. Your palace never changes, and a route like a speedrun is fixed down to the order of each step.

A speedrun is simply one well-defined walk through that world. Runners repeat the same path thousands of times, and the video gives you a precise reference you can rewatch to fix the order of the stops.

### The route

The world-record route starts with a Hunter carrying the Master Key, kills the Asylum Demon with a longsword and heads to Firelink Shrine to pick up humanity and firebombs before going down towards New Londo.[^3] From that opening, we can take ten loci:

| # | Locus | What's there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **The cell** | Where you wake up, hollow and locked in. |
| 2 | **The hole in the ceiling** | Where a body drops through with the key to your cell. |
| 3 | **The courtyard bonfire** | The first bonfire you light. |
| 4 | **The Asylum Demon's landing** | The great hall where the demon crashes down and you flee through a side gate. |
| 5 | **The longsword** | The weapon the route picks up on the way. |
| 6 | **The stairs up** | The climb back up through the asylum. |
| 7 | **The balcony** | The ledge above the demon, where you jump for the plunging attack. |
| 8 | **The arena floor** | The fight itself. |
| 9 | **The raven** | The giant crow that carries you out of the asylum. |
| 10 | **Firelink Shrine** | The bonfire at the heart of the game. |

If you don't play Dark Souls, pick another game with the same three properties: a large, hand-built world that is identical on every playthrough. Avoid procedurally generated games, where the layout changes each run. A speedrun video is a useful way to fix the route in a stable order: watch it a few times and name each landmark aloud.

### Using it: the first ten elements

Let's memorise the first ten elements of the periodic table: hydrogen, helium, lithium, beryllium, boron, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine and neon. The rule is to make each image vivid, exaggerated and tied to its location.

1. **The cell — hydrogen.** A burning airship, like the Hindenburg, is crammed into your cell. You wake up next to it.
2. **The hole in the ceiling — helium.** Instead of falling, the body floats down slowly on a bunch of party balloons, complaining in a squeaky voice.
3. **The bonfire — lithium.** You throw a handful of phone batteries on the bonfire. It flares crimson and pops.
4. **The demon's landing — beryllium.** The Asylum Demon crashes through the roof, and its eyes are two enormous emeralds (beryl). You run.
5. **The longsword — boron.** A wild boar is chewing on the longsword. You have to pry it out of its tusks.
6. **The stairs — carbon.** Every step is made of charcoal. Your hands and boots are black by the time you reach the top, and there's a diamond on the last step.
7. **The balcony — nitrogen.** Freezing white fog of liquid nitrogen pours over the edge. You jump through it.
8. **The arena floor — oxygen.** The demon is wheezing and gasping for air, and you hold your breath as you swing.
9. **The raven — fluorine.** The crow grins at you with blindingly white, freshly fluoridated teeth.
10. **Firelink Shrine — neon.** A glowing orange neon sign above the bonfire reads "OPEN 24/7".

Now close your eyes and run the route. Wake up in the cell, look up at the ceiling, light the bonfire, and so on. With a little practice, you should be able to recite the elements forwards and backwards, and say instantly that the seventh is nitrogen, because the seventh place is the balcony.

## Does it improve memory?

Yes, by a substantial amount, at least for the kind of material it's designed for.

**The meta-analyses.** A 2021 meta-analysis pooled 13 randomised controlled trials, mostly with university students, and found a medium-sized benefit for recall (Hedges' g = 0.65, 95% CI 0.45–0.85). The result held up after adjusting for publication bias and removing individual studies.[^4] A larger systematic review in 2025 found an even bigger effect on immediate serial recall compared with simple rehearsal (d = 0.88, 95% CI 0.47–1.25), but rated the risk of bias in many of the included trials as high.[^5] The direction is clear; the exact size under everyday conditions is less certain.

**Ordinary people can learn it.** In the best-known training study, researchers compared 23 of the world's top memory athletes with matched controls, then trained 51 young men with no mnemonic experience.[^6] They were randomly assigned to six weeks of method of loci training (40 half-hour sessions), six weeks of working-memory training, or no training.

- Before training, participants recalled roughly 26–30 words from a 72-word list.
- After six weeks, the loci group roughly doubled that, approaching the level of the memory athletes.
- Four months later, without further training, they were still performing at a similar level.
- The working-memory and no-training groups showed no comparable gains.

**The memories last.** Memory competitions test recall minutes after study, which leaves open whether the method produces lasting memories or just short-lived ones. A reanalysis of the same training study separated "weak" memories, recalled after 20 minutes but forgotten by the next day, from "durable" ones, still there 24 hours later.[^7] Loci training increased durable memories, not weak ones, and better performance after four months was partly associated with the training.

**It works in older adults too, with a big catch.** Healthy middle-aged and older adults trained in the method improve their memory.[^8] But using it takes effort. In the large ACTIVE trial of older adults, many participants trained in mnemonics found it hard to keep using the method of loci under time pressure, and only about a quarter made an effort to use it in follow-up sessions over five years.[^9] A technique you stop using can't help you.

## What does it do to the brain?

This is where the method gets interesting, and where claims about "brain health" need care.

**Network changes, not bigger brain regions.** Memory athletes' brains didn't differ structurally from controls in either the 2003 or 2017 studies. The differences were in functional connectivity: how strongly brain regions coordinate their activity at rest. In Dresler's study, six weeks of loci training shifted participants' connectivity patterns towards those that distinguish memory athletes from everyone else, and the closer the resemblance, the better their memory four months later.[^6] Neither control group showed this shift.

**More efficient processing.** You might expect better memory to come with more brain activity. The opposite happened: after training, people showed less activation in several regions during learning, a pattern also seen in memory athletes. The researchers interpret this as neural efficiency. Trained participants also showed stronger coupling between the hippocampus and neocortex after learning, during the period when memories are being consolidated, and this was stronger in people who formed more durable memories.[^7]

**Cortical thickness in older adults.** In a Norwegian study, middle-aged and older adults who did eight weeks of loci training showed regional increases in cortical thickness compared with untrained controls.[^8] Thickening in the right fusiform and lateral orbitofrontal cortex correlated with how much their memory improved. The authors noted that this was a short-term study and couldn't show whether training changes the long-term trajectory of the ageing brain.

### Is that "brain health"?

Not in the sense of protecting against decline or dementia, at least not yet. These studies show that the brain reorganises as you learn a demanding skill, which is what you'd expect from any intensive learning. None measured long-term decline.

The best long-term evidence comes from the ACTIVE trial, which randomised 2,832 older adults to ten sessions of memory training (mnemonic strategies for lists and text), reasoning training, speed training or no training.[^10] After ten years:

- Reasoning and speed training still improved their target abilities.
- **Memory training effects on memory performance were no longer detectable.**
- All three training groups, including memory, reported less difficulty with everyday activities than controls.

A 20-year follow-up linked to Medicare records found lower dementia diagnoses after speed training with booster sessions, but not significantly after memory training (covered in more detail in my [cognitive health guide](/posts/20261101_cognitive-health-guide/)).[^11] Even Dresler doubted that loci training would do much better or worse than other cognitive training regimes at preventing decline.

## Does it transfer?

Transfer is the question of whether practising one skill improves another. Researchers distinguish **near transfer**, to similar tasks, from **far transfer**, to different abilities like reasoning, attention or general intelligence.

**Near transfer is real.** The method of loci isn't tied to one kind of list. Once you've learned it, you can use it for words, numbers, names, faces or the points of a talk. Memory athletes adapt it to memorise playing cards and binary digits. That generality is a big part of its value.

**Far transfer is not.** Several lines of evidence point the same way:

- **Memory champions aren't smarter.** Their general cognitive ability matched controls; their advantage was strategy.[^1] Memory athletes are as prone as anyone to forgetting their keys.
- **The skill stays in its lane.** In Dresler's study, the brain and memory gains appeared only in the loci group, not in the group that spent the same six weeks on a working-memory task.
- **Cognitive training in general rarely transfers.** A second-order meta-analysis, pooling many meta-analyses of working-memory training, music, chess, video games and exergames, found that training reliably improves the trained task and similar ones, but that far-transfer effects were small or null across populations and programmes.[^12] The authors later argued that the overall far-transfer effect across the field is essentially zero once study quality and sampling error are accounted for.[^13]

Mnemonics are best understood as a tool, like a calculator for memory. A tool that works on any list is valuable. But practising with it doesn't upgrade the underlying hardware in a way that shows up elsewhere.

There's one more limit worth naming. The method of loci is excellent for arbitrary, ordered information. It does little for understanding. Memorising the first ten elements is not the same as knowing why fluorine is so reactive. For material you need to understand and apply, techniques like retrieval practice and spaced repetition are better supported, and mnemonics work best as a supplement for the parts that really are arbitrary.

## How much weight can this evidence bear?

- **Strong:** the method of loci improves recall of lists, with consistent medium-to-large effects across trials. Naive learners can acquire it in weeks, and the gains last months.
- **Promising but early:** brain changes after training, including connectivity patterns resembling memory athletes', more efficient processing and cortical thickening. The training studies are small (17 trained participants in Dresler's study; all young men) and measured change over weeks to months.
- **Weak or absent:** far transfer to intelligence or general cognition, and protection against age-related decline or dementia.
- **Practical caveat:** the method takes effort, and many people, especially older adults, stop using it.

## What this means for you

| Goal | Use the method of loci? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Remember a speech, list or sequence | Yes | This is what it's built for. |
| Memorise numbers, cards, names | Yes, with extra systems | Combine with the major system or name-face images. |
| Learn and understand a subject | As a supplement | Use retrieval practice and spacing for understanding. |
| Become smarter overall | No | No good evidence of far transfer. |
| Protect your ageing brain | Not on current evidence | Memory training effects faded in ACTIVE; see the cognitive health guide for better-supported steps. |
| Enjoy a mental challenge | Sure | It's fun, and harmless. |

If you want to try it, start small. Pick a route you know as well as the opening of a Dark Souls speedrun, use it for ten items this week, and see how many you can still recall next week.
