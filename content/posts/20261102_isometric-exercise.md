Lean against a wall, slide down until your thighs burn, and hold for two minutes. Rest for two minutes. Do it four times. That is 14 minutes, three times a week, and it has become one of the best-studied ways to lower blood pressure without medication.

Isometric exercise means contracting a muscle without visibly changing its length: holding a wall squat, squeezing a handgrip, pushing against an immovable bar. It used to be a rehab tool and a curiosity from 1950s German strength research. In the last few years it has picked up a large body of trial evidence, mostly for blood pressure, and a smaller but useful body of evidence for strength, muscle size and tendon health.

This post covers what the studies did, what changed, how big the effects were, and how to turn the protocols into something you can actually do.

## What counts as isometric exercise?

The muscle produces force, but the joint stays still. There are two common styles:

- **Holding (yielding) isometrics.** You resist a load and stop it moving: a wall squat, a plank, holding a dumbbell with your elbow at 90°. Most blood pressure research uses this style.
- **Pushing (overcoming) isometrics.** You push or pull against something that cannot move: a bar pinned in a rack, a strap, a wall. This lets you produce near-maximal force, which matters for strength and tendon work.

The three exercises that dominate the research are:

- **Isometric wall squat.** Back flat against a wall, lower legs vertical, knees bent to a set angle.
- **Isometric handgrip.** Squeezing a handgrip dynamometer at a fixed percentage of your maximum, usually 30%.
- **Isometric leg extension.** Pushing against a fixed knee-extension machine, mostly used in labs.

Planks are isometric too, but there is much less training data on them.

### What about carries, crawls and get-ups?

Many popular exercises contain isometric work without being isometric exercises.

- **Planks** are genuine isometric holds: the whole body stays still. They raise blood pressure during the hold much as wall sits do, but I'm not aware of any trial testing plank training for resting blood pressure.[^1]
- **Carries** (farmer's, suitcase, overhead) come closest among the moving exercises. Your grip, upper back, shoulders and trunk hold a fixed position under load. But your legs are walking, so the exercise as a whole is dynamic. Coaches often call them "loaded isometrics" for the trunk and grip.
- **Crawls** (bear crawl, leopard crawl) are a form of walking on all fours. The isometric part is the trunk, which has to resist sagging and twisting while the arms and legs move.
- **Get-ups** move the whole body from lying to standing and back. The main isometric element is the arm holding the weight, with the elbow locked and the shoulder stabilising the load overhead.

This matters for interpreting the research. The blood pressure trials used one sustained position, the same muscles contracting continuously for two minutes, at a controlled intensity: a fixed wall squat height or a set percentage of maximum grip. Carries, crawls and get-ups don't fit that model. Muscles switch on and off, intensity isn't standardised, and the moving parts change the cardiovascular response. A heavy farmer's carry is probably the nearest relative of handgrip training, but none of these exercises has been tested for blood pressure, so the 5–8 mmHg findings below shouldn't be assumed to transfer.

For strength and tendons the line is blurrier. A long, heavy carry is a real isometric stimulus for the grip and trunk, and the overhead hold in a get-up loads the shoulder in a similar way. The general principles later in this post (enough intensity, long enough holds) plausibly apply, though without direct trials behind them.

## Effect 1: blood pressure

This is where the evidence is strongest, and where the headlines came from.

### The big network meta-analysis

**Edwards et al. (2023), *British Journal of Sports Medicine*.**[^2] The research group behind most wall squat studies, at Canterbury Christ Church University in the UK, pooled 270 randomised trials with 15,827 participants. They compared aerobic exercise, dynamic resistance training, combined training, high-intensity interval training (HIIT) and isometric training against no exercise. Every type of exercise lowered resting blood pressure. Isometric training lowered it the most: by 8.24 mmHg systolic and 4.00 mmHg diastolic. For comparison, aerobic training lowered it by 4.49/2.53 mmHg and HIIT by 4.08/2.50 mmHg. Among individual exercises, the wall squat ranked first for systolic pressure and running first for diastolic.

Two caveats matter. Isometric trials were far fewer and smaller than aerobic trials, and many came from the same few labs. And a network meta-analysis ranks exercises partly through indirect comparisons, since few trials pitted isometric training directly against cardio.

### A newer, isometric-only meta-analysis

**Yan et al. (2026), *Frontiers in Public Health*.**[^3] Forty randomised trials of isometric training (25 handgrip, 8 wall squat, 6 isometric leg training), with about 1,700 participants. Isometric training lowered systolic pressure by 6.62 mmHg (95% CI 5.10 to 8.13) and diastolic by 2.63 mmHg (95% CI 1.76 to 3.50), rated moderate certainty. Subgroups pointed in the expected directions:

- **Wall squat beat handgrip:** −10.3/−5.3 mmHg for wall squats against −5.2/−2.0 mmHg for handgrip.
- **People with hypertension gained most:** −8.2 mmHg systolic, against −4.6 mmHg in healthy adults.
- **Three sessions a week** was the most common and best-performing frequency.

These are between-study comparisons, so they are hints rather than proof, and the authors found signs of small-study effects for systolic pressure (smaller trials reporting bigger reductions). The true average effect may be a little smaller than the headline.

### Wall squat vs handgrip, head to head

**Edwards et al. (2026), *European Journal of Applied Physiology*.**[^4] Twenty-one healthy, inactive adults (average age 33) did 4 weeks of home-based wall squats and 4 weeks of home-based handgrip training in random order, separated by a 4-week washout. Both used 4 × 2 minutes with 2 minutes of rest, three times a week. Wall squats lowered blood pressure by 6.8/4.8 mmHg and handgrip by 4.8/2.5 mmHg. The difference in systolic pressure was not significant; the wall squat lowered diastolic and mean pressure significantly more. There was no non-exercising control group, adherence was over 85%, and nobody had an adverse event.

### Does it work at home?

The early isometric trials were supervised in labs. The obvious question is whether people can do it alone.

**Home wall squats.**[^5] Twenty-eight healthy men with normal blood pressure did 4 weeks of home wall squats and 4 weeks of nothing, in random order. Each session was 4 × 2 minutes at a knee angle set in the lab to push heart rate to 95% of peak, with 2 minutes of rest, three times a week. Blood pressure fell by 4/3 mmHg and resting heart rate by 5 beats a minute.

**Home handgrip, a warning.**[^6] Seventy-two people with treated hypertension (average age around 60, 70% women) were randomised to supervised handgrip training, the same training at home, or a control group for 12 weeks (4 × 2 minutes at 30% of maximum, alternating hands, 1 minute of rest, three times a week). The supervised group's systolic pressure fell from 132 to 120 mmHg. The home group's barely changed. The authors could not say whether home participants squeezed less hard, skipped sessions or both.

So home training can work, but the effect seems to depend on actually hitting the intensity. The more recent home wall squat trials handle this with careful intensity cues (see the programming section below).

### How long does it last, and how often do you need to train?

**One year of wall squats.**[^7] Twenty-four unmedicated men with high-normal blood pressure were randomised to a year of home wall squat training or no training. Compared with controls, the wall squat group's resting blood pressure was lower by 8.5 mmHg systolic and 7.3 mmHg diastolic at the end of the year. Small, all-male and single-centre, but it is the longest isometric trial available and the effect did not fade.

**Frequency and detraining.**[^8] One hundred inactive adults (average age 37) with normal to high-normal blood pressure were randomised to a control group or to 4 weeks of home wall squats three times a week. For the next 4 weeks, the trained participants either kept going three times a week, dropped to twice or once a week, or stopped.

- All training groups lowered blood pressure in the first 4 weeks.
- At 8 weeks, the three-times-a-week group had the largest drop in systolic pressure from baseline (−13.7 mmHg). The twice-weekly (−7.0 mmHg) and once-weekly (−6.6 mmHg) groups held on to a smaller but still significant systolic reduction compared with controls.
- The group that stopped drifted back to control levels within 4 weeks.
- No reduced-frequency group kept a significant diastolic reduction.

The maintenance phase lasted only 4 weeks, so we do not know how long a once-a-week habit holds the effect. The practical reading: the benefit needs ongoing training, three sessions a week is best, and one or two is much better than none.

### Why might it work?

Nobody is sure. During a hard hold, the contracting muscle squeezes its own blood vessels, blood pressure and heart rate rise sharply, and metabolites build up. When you let go, blood rushes back in. Repeated over weeks, this seems to change how blood vessels and the autonomic nervous system regulate pressure. Mechanistic studies point variously to lower resting heart rate, lower peripheral resistance and better autonomic balance, and the main driver may differ between healthy people and people with hypertension.[^4] The bigger effect of wall squats is usually explained by the larger muscle mass involved.

### How big is 5–8 mmHg?

The researchers behind the wall squat trials describe reductions of this size as comparable to a single standard-dose blood pressure drug, and a large analysis of drug trials found that every 5 mmHg drop in systolic pressure lowers the risk of major cardiovascular events by about 10% (both cited in Edwards et al., 2026).[^4] Isometric training has not been tested against hard outcomes like heart attacks or strokes. The blood pressure drop is a surrogate, but it is a well-validated one.

## Effect 2: strength

Isometric training builds strength. The questions are how much, and whether that strength carries over to moving.

**Isometric vs dynamic training.**[^9] A meta-analysis of 32 studies with 621 healthy adults aged 18–45. Isometric training improved strength more than no training (SMD 0.65, a moderate effect). Compared with conventional dynamic weight training, it produced larger gains in isometric strength (SMD 0.43) but similar gains in isokinetic strength, which is measured while the limb moves at a set speed. In other words, isometric training is very good at making you strong at holding, and roughly as good as dynamic training at a moving strength test.

**The catch: angle specificity.** Strength gains from isometrics are greatest at the trained joint angle.

- **Kitai & Sale (1989).**[^10] Six women trained their calf muscles at a single ankle angle with 2 sets of ten 5-second maximal contractions, three times a week for 6 weeks. Strength rose only at the training angle and the angles 5° either side.
- **Lanza et al. (2019).**[^11] Thirteen people trained knee extension at 65° of knee bend for 14 sessions over 4 weeks. Strength rose by 12% at 65°, 11% at 50°, 7% at 80° and only 5% at 35°. Gains spread to nearby angles, but tapered away from the trained one.

**Folland et al. (2005)** showed a simple fix.[^12] Thirty-three men trained one leg isometrically at four different knee angles and the other leg with ordinary lifting and lowering, for 9 weeks. Moving (isokinetic) strength rose by the same amount in both legs: 10.5% and 10.7%. Isometric strength rose more in the isometric leg (18% vs 13%). Spread the holds across several angles and you lose little compared with dynamic lifting.

**Ballistic intent.** In a systematic review of 26 studies, Oranchuk et al. (2019) found that trying to reach peak force as fast as possible ("ballistic" isometrics), rather than ramping up gradually, produced bigger gains in rapid force production and muscle activation.[^13] That matters for sport and for older adults, where how fast you can produce force affects things like catching a fall.

## Effect 3: muscle size

Isometric training grows muscle, and joint angle matters here too.

The same Oranchuk review found that holds at **longer muscle lengths** (the muscle stretched, e.g. a deep knee bend for the quadriceps) produced more growth than the same volume at short lengths: roughly 0.9–1.7% per week against 0.1–0.8% per week.[^13] Long-length training also carried over better to dynamic performance. Muscle growth and maximal strength improved across a wide range of intensities, so you do not need maximal efforts to grow muscle.

In Kubo et al. (2001), described below, 12 weeks of isometric knee extensions at 70% of maximum increased quadriceps volume by about 7.5%, which is comparable to many dynamic training studies of similar length.[^14]

## Effect 4: tendons

Tendons get stiffer and stronger with heavy loading, and isometric holds are a convenient way to load them.

**Long vs short holds.**[^14] Eight young men trained knee extension at 70% of maximum, four days a week for 12 weeks. One leg did long holds (4 × 20 seconds, with 1 minute of rest); the other did many brief contractions (3 sets of 50 one-second contractions). Both legs gained about a third in strength and about 7.5% in muscle volume. But tendon stiffness increased only in the long-hold leg.

Across studies, Oranchuk et al. (2019) concluded that **high intensity, at least 70% of maximum, is required** to change tendon structure and function.[^13] Low-intensity holds, like the wall squats used for blood pressure, are unlikely to do much for tendons.

## Effect 5: tendon pain (more contested than you might think)

Isometrics became popular in physiotherapy after one striking study.

**Rio et al. (2015).**[^15] Six volleyball players with patellar tendon pain did five 45-second holds at 70% of maximal knee extension, with 2 minutes of rest. Pain during a single-leg decline squat fell from about 7/10 to almost zero, stayed low for at least 45 minutes, and strength rose by about 19%. A bout of slow lifting and lowering produced much less pain relief.

**Then the replications.**

- In a pre-registered replication with 20 people with patellar tendinopathy, using the same methods, isometrics were no better than dynamic exercise.[^16]
- In 20 people with plantar fasciopathy, isometrics were no better than dynamic exercise or walking for immediate pain relief.[^17]
- In jumping athletes training in-season, 4 weeks of isometric or dynamic exercise both reduced tendon pain, with no difference between them.[^18]

A group of tendon researchers summed up the evidence as too thin to make isometrics the default first step.[^19] The fair reading: isometric holds are a useful, low-irritation way to keep loading a painful tendon, and some people feel immediate relief, but they are not a special painkiller. Progressing to heavier, slower dynamic loading remains the core of tendon rehab.

## Safety: what happens to blood pressure during the hold?

This is the main reason guidelines have been slow to recommend isometric training. Blood pressure rises steeply during a hard isometric hold, and more so with big muscles like the legs.

**Jones et al. (2026), *Journal of Hypertension*.**[^1] Sixty-two healthy adults (average age 27, resting blood pressure 121/77 mmHg) held a plank and a wall sit to exhaustion while blood pressure was monitored beat by beat. Testing stopped if pressure crossed American College of Sports Medicine safety limits (systolic over 250 or diastolic over 115 mmHg). Almost everyone (97–98%) crossed the diastolic limit, after about 50 seconds on average. No one reached the systolic limit and no one had an adverse event.

So a normal hard hold sends diastolic pressure into ranges that exercise testing guidelines treat as a stopping point. In healthy young people this appears to be harmless. In people with uncontrolled hypertension, aortic disease, a recent cardiac event or certain eye conditions, it may not be. The training trials mostly excluded such people, and the ones that included hypertensive patients generally supervised them or screened them carefully.

Practical points:

- **Breathe throughout.** Holding your breath (the Valsalva manoeuvre) adds to the pressure spike. Every protocol in the trials instructs normal breathing.
- **Handgrip is gentler.** It produces a much smaller rise in blood pressure than the wall squat, so it may suit people who are older, frail, have knee problems or are at higher cardiovascular risk.[^4]
- **Get clearance first** if you have high blood pressure that isn't controlled, any heart or vascular condition, or have had eye surgery or glaucoma.

## How to program isometric training

The table below translates the study protocols into practical setups. It is a reading of the research, not a set of tested prescriptions for every goal.

| Goal | Example setup | Based on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lower blood pressure | Wall squat, 4 × 2 min, 2 min seated rest, 3 days a week (at least 48 h apart). Effort building from about 4/10 to 8.5/10 across the four holds | Wiles 2017; Edwards 2023, 2026; Swift 2026 |
| Lower blood pressure, gentler | Handgrip, 4 × 2 min at 30% of maximum, alternating hands, 1–2 min rest, 3 days a week | Farah 2018; Edwards 2026 |
| Keep a blood pressure gain when busy | Drop to 1–2 wall squat sessions a week rather than stopping | Swift 2026 |
| Maximal strength | Near-maximal pushes of 3–5 s, e.g. 2 sets of 10, 3 days a week, at 3–4 joint angles | Kitai & Sale 1989; Folland 2005 |
| Rapid force (power) | Same, but hit peak force as fast as possible on each rep | Oranchuk 2019 |
| Muscle size | Holds at long muscle lengths (deep joint angles); intensity can be moderate | Oranchuk 2019 |
| Tendon stiffness | Holds at 70%+ of maximum for 20–45 s, about 4–5 sets, 3–4 days a week | Kubo 2001; Oranchuk 2019 |
| Painful tendon | 5 × 45 s at about 70%, 2 min rest, as a bridge to heavier dynamic loading | Rio 2015 (mixed replications) |

### Setting wall squat intensity without a lab

The trials set wall squat intensity in two ways. The original method used a lab test to find the knee angle that drove heart rate to 95% of peak. Newer home trials use effort ratings instead, which anyone can do:[^20][^4]

1. Stand with your back flat against a wall and walk your feet out so your lower legs are vertical when you squat.
2. Slide down to a height you think would feel like about **4 out of 10** effort after two minutes, and mark it on the wall.
3. Hold four 2-minute bouts with 2 minutes of seated rest between them. Aim for effort at the end of each bout of roughly **4, 5.5, 7 and 8.5 out of 10**. Effort should climb as fatigue builds; the height stays the same.
4. If your rating falls outside those targets, move the mark up or down by 1–2 cm next session.

A lower squat is harder. Do not go below thighs parallel to the floor, and keep your knees over your ankles. The last bout should be genuinely hard, not comfortable.

### Setting handgrip intensity

Handgrip protocols need a digital handgrip dynamometer (they are inexpensive). Squeeze as hard as you can three times, take the best value, and train at 30% of it, watching the display to stay on target. Spring-type grippers are harder to dose precisely.

### A sample week

For someone who wants the blood pressure benefit plus some strength:

- **Monday, Wednesday, Friday:** wall squat, 4 × 2 minutes (14 minutes).
- **Tuesday, Thursday:** 10–15 minutes of pushing isometrics for strength, e.g. a split-squat hold, a push against a doorframe and a pull against a strap, each at 2–3 joint angles, with 5-second near-maximal efforts.

This complements rather than replaces aerobic exercise. None of the isometric trials set out to improve cardiorespiratory fitness, which is one of the strongest predictors of long-term health.

## How much weight can this evidence bear?

The blood pressure findings are consistent, but the evidence has clear limits.

- **Small trials.** Most isometric trials had 10–30 people per group. The largest pooled analyses still rest on fewer than 2,000 participants.
- **One research hub.** A large share of the wall squat trials come from one UK university group. Their results are consistent, but independent replication matters.
- **Short durations.** Most interventions lasted 4–12 weeks. The one-year trial had 24 participants, all men.
- **Supervision matters.** Supervised handgrip worked; home-based handgrip, in one trial, did not. The home wall squat trials were carefully monitored, with diaries and effort targets.
- **Surrogate outcome.** All of this is about blood pressure readings, not heart attacks, strokes or deaths.
- **Possible overestimation.** At least one meta-analysis found signs that small positive studies are over-represented.
- **Strength and tendon evidence is thinner.** The angle-specificity and tendon studies are often tiny (6–13 people) and short.

## What this means for you

If your blood pressure is high-normal or mildly raised, the wall squat is one of the most time-efficient, best-supported things you can try: about 42 minutes a week, no equipment, measurable results within a month. Measure your blood pressure before and after a few weeks to see whether it works for you. If your blood pressure is high or you take medication, talk to your doctor first, and do not stop medication because a number improves.

For strength, isometrics are a good complement to lifting, especially when a joint is painful through part of its range, when you have no equipment, or when you want to load a tendon without much movement. Train at several angles, or at long muscle lengths, and push hard.

Whatever the goal, breathe throughout and keep going. The benefits fade within weeks of stopping.
