Intermittent fasting has gone from fringe to mainstream in about a decade. The claims range from modest ("it's an easy way to eat less") to grand ("it resets your metabolism, triggers autophagy and slows ageing"). Over the past few years, enough randomized trials have finished to give a fairly clear answer: **fasting works about as well as ordinary calorie restriction, and mostly for the same reason. It's a structure for eating less.** Some formats may have a small edge, some people find it much easier to stick to, and a few groups should avoid it entirely.

This post covers the main protocols, what the best trials show, autophagy, whether fasting does anything beyond calorie restriction, what does and doesn't break a fast, the pros and cons, fasted exercise, and the minimal effective dose.

*This post is general information, not medical advice. If you take medication for diabetes or blood pressure, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have a history of disordered eating, talk to your doctor before fasting.*

## The main forms of fasting

"Intermittent fasting" is an umbrella term. The research usually splits it into three families: time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, and whole-day fasting such as 5:2[^1].

| Protocol | How it works | Typical fast-day intake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Time-restricted eating (TRE), e.g. 16:8** | Eat within a daily window (often 6–10 h), fast the rest | No restriction inside the window |
| **Early TRE (eTRE)** | Window placed early, e.g. 8 am–2 pm | No restriction inside the window |
| **5:2** | Eat normally 5 days, restrict 2 non-consecutive days | ~500–600 kcal |
| **4:3** | Eat normally 4 days, restrict 3 non-consecutive days | ~20% of energy needs |
| **Alternate-day fasting (ADF)** | Alternate fast days and normal or "feast" days | 0–25% of energy needs |
| **OMAD** | One meal a day (an extreme form of TRE) | One meal |
| **Prolonged / periodic fasting** | Multi-day fasts or a "fasting-mimicking diet" a few times a year | Water only, or ~750–1,100 kcal |

Most "fast days" in the trials aren't true zero-calorie fasts. 5:2, 4:3 and most ADF studies allow a small meal on fast days, which is why the literature prefers the term *intermittent energy restriction*.

## Weight loss: what the trials show

### The big picture

The best summary of the field is a 2025 network meta-analysis in *The BMJ*. It pooled 99 randomized trials with 6,582 adults (average BMI 31, nearly 90% with an existing health condition), lasting 3 to 52 weeks[^1]. Its findings:

- Every intermittent fasting strategy, and ordinary continuous calorie restriction, reduced weight compared with eating freely[^1].
- Alternate-day fasting was the only form to beat continuous calorie restriction, and only by about 1.3 kg, rated as moderate-certainty evidence. The edge showed up mainly in shorter trials[^1].
- Time-restricted eating and whole-day fasting (5:2) performed about the same as calorie restriction[^1].
- There were no differences for HbA1c (long-term blood sugar) or HDL cholesterol between fasting, calorie restriction and unrestricted diets[^1].

The authors called the improvements "trivial to small" and asked for longer trials[^1]. In short, fasting is a valid option, not a superior one.

### Key trials, one by one

| Trial | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trepanowski et al., *JAMA Intern Med* 2017[^2] | 100 adults with obesity, 1 year: ADF vs daily 25% calorie restriction vs control | Similar weight loss (about 6% vs 5.3%). ADF had the highest dropout (38%) and higher LDL cholesterol at 12 months |
| Lowe et al. (TREAT), *JAMA Intern Med* 2020[^3] | 116 adults, 12 weeks: 16:8 TRE (noon–8 pm) vs three meals a day, no calorie advice | 1.17% vs 0.75% weight loss, not significantly different; TRE group lost more appendicular lean mass |
| Liu et al., *NEJM* 2022[^5] | 139 adults with obesity, 1 year: 8 am–4 pm TRE plus calorie restriction vs calorie restriction alone | 8.0 kg vs 6.3 kg, not statistically significant |
| Lin et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2023[^6] | 90 adults with obesity, 1 year: 8-h TRE without counting vs 25% calorie restriction vs control | TRE −4.6 kg and CR −5.4 kg vs control; TRE vs CR not significantly different |
| Catenacci et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2025[^7] | 165 adults, 1 year, with intensive behavioural support: 4:3 vs daily calorie restriction matched for weekly deficit | 7.6% vs 5.0% weight loss, favouring 4:3 |

A few things stand out:

- **When calories are matched, timing adds little.** Liu's NEJM trial is the cleanest test: both groups had the same calorie target, and adding an eating window didn't produce significantly more weight loss[^5]. Most of the effect comes from eating less.
- **TRE without calorie control is weak.** In TREAT, the 16:8 group barely lost more than people eating three meals a day[^3]. In Lin's trial, TRE did work against a control group, with people cutting roughly 400 kcal a day without counting, which matched the calorie restriction group[^6]. The difference may be that Lin's participants had obesity and support, whereas TREAT was a light-touch app study.
- **The 4:3 result is the most favourable long trial so far.** It's a single trial, with heavy behavioural support for both groups, so the 2.6-percentage-point edge needs replicating[^7]. It does fit the BMJ finding that ADF-style schedules may have a slight advantage[^1].
- **Adherence is the deciding factor, and it varies by person.** ADF had more dropouts than daily restriction in Trepanowski's trial[^2], while TRE was easy to stick with in Lin's[^6]. A 2025 review by Michelle Harvie, one of the researchers behind the 5:2 diet, concluded that 5:2 and ADF have long-term adherence, weight loss and metabolic benefits equivalent to daily restriction[^9]. In one of her earlier trials, in 115 women, two low-calorie, low-carbohydrate days a week reduced body fat more than daily restriction (about 3.7 kg vs 2.0 kg)[^8].

## Beyond weight: metabolic health

### Insulin sensitivity and the "meal timing" question

The most cited mechanistic study is Sutton et al. (2018). In a tightly controlled crossover trial, men with prediabetes ate either within a 6-hour early window (dinner before 3 pm) or a 12-hour window for five weeks each, with enough food to keep their weight stable. Early TRE improved insulin sensitivity, beta cell responsiveness, blood pressure and oxidative stress, and reduced evening appetite, without weight loss[^10]. It was a small study of eight men, but it suggested that *when* you eat might matter on its own.

A larger and more recent test points the other way. The 2025 ChronoFast trial, from the German Institute of Human Nutrition and Charité Berlin, randomized 31 women with overweight or obesity in a crossover design to an 8-hour eating window, either early or late, with calories carefully held constant. After two weeks, there were no clinically relevant changes in insulin sensitivity, blood sugar, blood fats or inflammation markers. What did change was the timing of the body's internal clocks and sleep[^11].

Taken together: there may be a modest benefit from eating *earlier* in the day in some people, especially those with prediabetes, but the claim that a shorter eating window improves metabolism without eating less hasn't held up well in controlled trials. The 2025 BMJ meta-analysis also found that TRE slightly raised total and LDL cholesterol compared with whole-day fasting[^1].

### Type 2 diabetes

The most striking result comes from the Chinese EARLY trial, published in *JAMA Network Open* in 2024. It randomized 405 adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity to a 5:2 diet using meal replacements, metformin, or empagliflozin for 16 weeks[^12]. The 5:2 group had the largest HbA1c reduction, and about 76% kept HbA1c below 6.5% eight weeks after treatment ended[^12]. Weight loss averaged nearly 10 kg. The participants were early in their disease and not yet on medication, and the trial was open-label, so the result applies to that group rather than to everyone with diabetes.

### Prolonged fasting and fasting-mimicking diets

Valter Longo's group tested a fasting-mimicking diet (FMD): five days a month of a low-calorie, low-protein, plant-based diet, for three months, in 100 generally healthy adults[^16]. BMI, blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol improved, mainly in participants who were already at risk, and side effects were mild (fatigue, weakness, headaches)[^16]. The FMD is sold commercially by a company Longo founded, the control group simply ate as usual, and 71 people completed the study, so it's encouraging but not definitive.

### Autophagy: plausible, but barely tested in humans

Autophagy is the process cells use to break down and recycle damaged components, and it declines with age. In cells and animals, nutrient restriction is one of the most reliable ways to increase it, and a 2018 review concluded that fasting and calorie restriction induce autophagy across a wide range of tissues[^17]. Almost all of that evidence comes from animals and cell cultures, though.

**The measurement problem.** You can't routinely biopsy people's organs, and a lack of techniques to measure autophagy in humans has held the field back[^18]. Most human studies now use white blood cells as a proxy and measure *flux*, the rate at which cells actually recycle material, rather than a single snapshot of marker proteins. Older human studies relied on snapshots and gave contradictory signals.

**What the newer human studies show:**

| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Singh et al., 2025[^18] | 42 healthy adults; flux after a 12-hour overnight fast vs 1 hour after a high-protein meal | No change after the protein meal; short-term eating and fasting barely moved autophagy in blood cells |
| Bensalem et al., *J Physiol* 2025[^27] | 121 adults with obesity randomized to standard care, calorie restriction, or intermittent time-restricted eating | Time-restricted eating *may* increase autophagic flux (exploratory analysis); calorie restriction didn't differ significantly from standard care |
| Espinoza et al., *GeroScience* 2026[^28] | 30 healthy adults randomized to two 5-day fasting-mimicking diets (ProLon or an alternative formula) or normal diet | Only the ProLon group showed increased autophagic flux, alongside lower weight, fasting glucose and insulin resistance |

The Bensalem result is the most interesting, because it hints at an effect of fasting itself rather than just eating less. But the authors call it exploratory, and it was measured in blood cells, not muscle, liver or brain. The fasting-mimicking diet trial was small and sponsored by L-Nutra, the company that sells ProLon[^28], so it needs independent replication.

**What this means in practice:**

- Claims like "autophagy kicks in at hour 16" or "do a 72-hour fast for a deep clean" aren't based on human data. Nobody knows what duration of fasting, if any, is needed to raise autophagy meaningfully in people.
- Even where fasting raises autophagy in blood cells, no study has shown that this translates into better health or longer life.
- The tentative positive signal came from regular time-restricted eating, not from long fasts. If autophagy is your reason to fast, it isn't a reason to do multi-day fasts.

Autophagy is a plausible bonus of fasting with emerging but preliminary human support, not a proven benefit.

## Is there a benefit beyond calorie restriction?

This is the question that matters most for fasting's bigger claims. If fasting really "flips a metabolic switch", it should beat ordinary dieting when both deliver the same calories. Most trials can't answer that, because the fasting group usually ends up eating less too. A few were designed to separate the two.

### The most direct test: Templeman et al. (2021)

A team at the University of Bath ran the cleanest version of this experiment, published in *Science Translational Medicine*. They randomized 36 lean adults to three diets for three weeks[^26]:

| Group | What they did | Calorie deficit? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily calorie restriction | Ate 75% of their energy needs every day | Yes |
| Alternate-day fasting, matched deficit | Fasted every other day, ate 150% on feast days | Yes, the same as daily restriction |
| Alternate-day fasting, no deficit | Fasted every other day, ate 200% on feast days | No |

The daily restriction group lost the most weight and fat, about 1.6 kg of fat on average. The alternate-day group with the same deficit lost less fat, and the researchers concluded that alternate-day fasting may offer no fasting-specific health or metabolic benefits over a standard daily diet[^26]. The fasting-without-deficit group was the key control: fasting on its own, with calories made up on feast days, did little. One reason the fasters did worse was behavioural: they tended to move less on fasting days, which partly offset their deficit[^26].

The limits: it lasted only three weeks, used lean, healthy people, and was small. It doesn't rule out benefits in people with obesity or prediabetes, or over longer periods. But it's the best direct evidence on the question.

### What the other calorie-matched trials show

- **Daily eating windows.** When calories were held constant, an 8-hour window didn't improve insulin sensitivity, blood sugar, blood fats or inflammation markers in the ChronoFast trial[^11]. When both groups had the same calorie target for a year, adding an 8-hour window didn't lead to significantly more weight loss[^5].
- **Early eating is the one partial exception.** Sutton's 6-hour early window improved insulin sensitivity and blood pressure without weight loss in men with prediabetes[^10], but the trial was tiny and ChronoFast didn't reproduce it. If timing helps on its own, it's probably about eating *earlier*, not fasting longer.
- **Body composition in some 5:2 trials.** Harvie's 5:2-style diets reduced body fat more than daily restriction[^8]. That hasn't been reproduced consistently, and the BMJ meta-analysis found only small differences between approaches[^1].
- **Cellular effects.** One exploratory trial suggests time-restricted eating may raise autophagy in blood cells where calorie restriction didn't[^27], but whether that matters for health is unknown (see the autophagy section above).

### So what is fasting good for?

The best evidence says fasting is a **different route to the same calorie deficit**, plus possibly a small bonus from eating earlier in the day. Its real advantage is behavioural. A fixed window cuts late-night snacking without any tracking[^6], and some people find two hard days a week easier than seven slightly hungry ones. If it's the route you can stick with, that alone makes it worthwhile. Just don't expect it to do anything a sustained calorie deficit wouldn't.

## What breaks a fast?

The honest answer is that it depends on why you're fasting. Most of the benefits in the trials above came from eating less, so for that purpose a fast is only "broken" by meaningful calories. The trials themselves used simple rules: TREAT's participants had to completely abstain from caloric intake outside their window[^3], while 5:2, 4:3 and ADF studies allow a small amount of food on fast days[^7][^9].

| Item | Breaks a fast for weight loss? | Breaks it for low insulin? | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Water, sparkling water | No | No | |
| Black coffee, plain tea | No | Practically no | A few calories per cup; allowed in most trials |
| Zero-calorie sweeteners and diet drinks | No | Possibly a little | See below |
| Electrolytes (salt, potassium, magnesium) | No | No | Useful on long fasts to prevent headaches |
| Splash of milk in coffee | Barely | Yes, slightly | Small amounts of carbs and protein |
| Bone broth, collagen, protein shakes | Yes (if more than a few dozen kcal) | Yes | Protein stimulates insulin |
| "Bulletproof" coffee (butter, MCT oil) | Yes | Not much | Often 200–400 kcal; it's a meal |
| Juice, sugary drinks | Yes | Yes | |
| Medications | No | No | Never skip prescribed medicines to "protect" a fast |

On sweeteners: a small crossover study by Yanina Pepino at Washington University gave 17 people with severe obesity either water or sucralose before a glucose drink. With sucralose, blood sugar peaked higher and insulin rose roughly 20% more than with water[^19]. That was sucralose *combined with* glucose, in people who didn't normally use sweeteners, so it doesn't show that a diet drink on its own breaks a fast. Regulators consider sucralose safe for glucose control[^19]. A practical reading: diet drinks won't undo a fast's calorie deficit, but if they make you hungrier or you're aiming for minimal insulin, skip them.

If you're doing a fasting-mimicking diet, the rules are set by the protocol: it deliberately allows around 750–1,100 kcal a day of specific foods.

## Pros and cons

### Pros

- **Works as well as standard dieting for weight loss.** That's a real benefit, not a consolation prize: having several effective options means more people find one they can stick with[^1].
- **Simplicity.** No calorie counting for TRE; Lin's participants cut about 400 kcal a day just by narrowing their window, largely by dropping after-dinner snacking[^6].
- **Possibly a small edge for ADF and 4:3.** Weekly-schedule approaches did slightly better than daily restriction in the BMJ meta-analysis and the 4:3 trial[^1][^7].
- **Type 2 diabetes.** A structured 5:2 plan produced large HbA1c and weight improvements in early type 2 diabetes[^12].
- **Early eating may help insulin sensitivity** in people with prediabetes, though the evidence is mixed[^10][^11].
- **Possibly more autophagy**, based on early, exploratory human data[^27].

### Cons

- **No magic.** Without eating less, a shorter window didn't improve insulin, blood sugar, lipids or inflammation in ChronoFast[^11], and fasting with the same calories as daily dieting led to less fat loss in Templeman's trial[^26].
- **Lean mass loss.** In TREAT, the 16:8 group lost more appendicular lean mass (arms and legs) than controls[^3]. The researchers estimated about 65% of the weight lost in that group was lean mass, versus a typical 20–30%, possibly because skipping breakfast lowered protein intake[^23]. Other researchers pointed out that the between-group difference was small, about 0.5 kg[^4]. Resistance training and adequate protein appear to prevent this (see below).
- **Dropout and hunger.** ADF had a 38% dropout rate in its one-year trial, the highest of the three groups[^2].
- **LDL cholesterol.** LDL rose more with ADF than with daily restriction at 12 months in Trepanowski's trial[^2], and TRE raised LDL slightly compared with whole-day fasting in the BMJ meta-analysis[^1].
- **Hormones in lean, trained men.** A 12-month trial of TRE plus resistance training found lower testosterone and IGF-1 than in the normal-diet group[^22], alongside lower fat mass and inflammation markers. The practical relevance is unclear.
- **Hypoglycaemia with diabetes medication.** In a New Zealand trial of 5:2 in type 2 diabetes, fast days doubled the rate of low blood sugar even with medication reduced and weekly supervision, though no severe episodes occurred[^13]. Anyone on insulin or sulfonylureas needs a medication plan from their doctor.
- **Disordered eating.** A survey of over 2,700 Canadian adolescents and young adults found intermittent fasting was associated with all disordered eating behaviours in women, including binge eating and vomiting, and with compulsive exercise in men[^14]. It's cross-sectional, so it can't show cause, but fasting is a poor idea for anyone with a history of an eating disorder.

### The 2024 "91% higher cardiovascular death" headline

In March 2024, an American Heart Association press release reported that people eating within an 8-hour window had a 91% higher risk of cardiovascular death, based on an abstract analysing about 20,000 US adults[^15]. It got enormous coverage. Critics, including a group of fasting and circadian researchers, pointed out it was an unpublished conference abstract, with eating windows estimated from just two days of dietary recall, and with people in the short-window group differing in other ways (more smokers, for example)[^15]. It's a weak observational signal that should prompt better studies, not a reason to panic. Randomized trials so far haven't shown cardiovascular harm, but none has run long enough to measure heart attacks or deaths.

### Who should avoid fasting (or only do it with medical supervision)

- People with a current or past eating disorder[^14]
- People on insulin or sulfonylureas, or with type 1 diabetes, unless working with their care team[^13]
- Pregnant or breastfeeding women
- Children and teenagers
- People who are underweight, frail or older with chronic illness, where losing lean mass is especially costly

## Fasted exercise

### Performance

A 2018 meta-analysis of 46 studies by Aird and colleagues found that eating before exercise improved performance in prolonged aerobic exercise (longer than about an hour), but made no difference for shorter aerobic sessions[^20]. If you're doing a long run, ride or match, eat first. For a 30–45-minute easy session, it doesn't matter much.

### Fat loss: "fasted cardio"

Exercising fasted does burn more fat *during* the session: the Aird meta-analysis found higher circulating free fatty acids after fasted exercise[^20]. But that doesn't mean more fat lost over weeks. In Schoenfeld et al. (2014), 20 young women on a calorie deficit did the same treadmill sessions for four weeks, either fasted or after a shake. Both groups lost weight and fat, with no difference between them[^21]. It was a short, small trial, but it matches the general finding: total energy balance matters far more than whether the stomach is empty during cardio.

### Metabolic adaptations

The interesting case for fasted training is metabolic, not fat-burning. In Van Proeyen et al. (2010), young men ate a high-calorie, high-fat diet for six weeks while doing endurance training either fasted or with carbohydrates before and during sessions. Only the fasted group improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, increased muscle GLUT4 (the glucose transporter) by 28%, and avoided significant weight gain[^21b]. It was a small study (about 10 people per group) under unusual conditions, but it suggests that training in a low-carbohydrate state may push muscle toward better fat use and glucose handling.

### Strength training and muscle

The worry is that fasting costs muscle. Trials combining time-restricted eating with resistance training are reassuring:

- **Moro et al. (2016):** 34 resistance-trained men did 8 weeks of 16:8 or a normal diet with the same training. The TRE group lost more fat, while fat-free mass, arm and thigh muscle area and maximal strength were maintained in both groups[^22].
- **Tinsley et al. (2017):** in young men starting resistance training, 8 weeks of TRE didn't impair lean mass retention or strength gains[^22b].
- **Moro et al. (2021):** a 12-month follow-up in 20 trained men found TRE was feasible and lowered body mass, fat mass and inflammatory markers, but also testosterone and IGF-1[^22].

Contrast those with TREAT, where people did TRE *without* structured training and lost more lean mass[^3]. The difference points to the practical rules: lift weights, and get enough protein across your eating window.

### Practical guidance

| Situation | Suggestion |
| --- | --- |
| Easy cardio under ~60 min | Fasted is fine if you feel good |
| Long endurance sessions or races | Eat beforehand; fed improves prolonged performance[^20] |
| Fasted cardio "for fat loss" | No advantage over fed cardio if calories are equal[^21] |
| Strength training while doing TRE | Fine; schedule protein-rich meals in your window and train regularly[^22] |
| High-intensity intervals | Try both; some people feel flat fasted |
| On glucose-lowering medication | Avoid fasted exercise unless your doctor has planned for it[^13] |

## The minimal effective dose

There isn't a proven minimum, because no fasting "dose" works on its own. In the trials, the benefit follows the calorie deficit the fast creates, not the number of hours without food. When calories are held constant, even an 8-hour window didn't improve insulin sensitivity or cardiovascular markers[^11]. That said, the evidence gives a reasonable floor.

**Daily eating window: about 10 hours (a 14-hour overnight fast).** This is the shortest version with any positive data. In a 2020 pilot study, 19 people with metabolic syndrome who usually ate over 14 hours or more a day limited themselves to a self-chosen 10-hour window for 12 weeks. They lost weight and lowered their blood pressure and atherogenic lipids, with no adverse effects, even though most were already on statins or blood pressure drugs[^24]. It was a single-arm study with no control group, so it shows a 10-hour window *can* help, but only if it actually shortens your eating day, mainly by cutting late-evening snacking. If you already eat within about 10 hours, a 10-hour window changes nothing.

**Going much shorter adds little.** Krista Varady's group compared 4-hour and 6-hour windows against a control group in adults with obesity. Both produced mild weight loss over 8 weeks[^25], without a dramatic difference between them. The year-long trials above used 8-hour windows and matched, rather than beat, ordinary calorie restriction[^5][^6].

**Whole-day fasting: 2 days a week.** 5:2 is the lowest dose with solid long-term trials[^9]. Moving up to 3 days (4:3) or alternate days seems to add a little[^7], but in the BMJ meta-analysis the extra weight loss from alternate-day fasting (about 1–1.7 kg compared with other approaches) stayed below the roughly 2 kg usually considered clinically meaningful[^1]. A single fast day a week (6:1) hasn't been tested well enough to call effective.

| Approach | Minimal effective dose | Strength of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time-restricted eating | ~10-hour window, if it shortens your usual eating day | Pilot data only for 10 h; randomized trials for 8 h |
| Whole-day fasting | 2 restricted days a week (5:2) | Randomized trials up to a year |
| Alternate-day / 4:3 | 3 restricted days a week | Randomized trials up to a year; slight edge |
| Prolonged fasting / FMD | No established minimum | A few small trials |

**In practice:** start with a consistent 10–12-hour eating window that ends a few hours before bed, and treat it as a way to eat less. If that doesn't change your weight or blood markers within a few months, step up to an 8-hour window or 5:2. Pushing to 16+ hours or multi-day fasts mostly adds hunger and dropout risk[^2], with little extra benefit, and there's no human evidence that longer fasts are needed for autophagy.

## Bottom line

| If you... | Then... |
| --- | --- |
| Want to lose weight | Any of these approaches works about as well as standard calorie restriction; pick the one you can sustain |
| Want the least effort | Start with a 10–12-hour eating window that cuts late snacking; step up only if needed |
| Hate counting calories | Try an 8–10-hour eating window, ideally earlier in the day |
| Prefer "on" and "off" days | Consider 5:2 or 4:3; 4:3 had the strongest one-year trial |
| Want metabolic benefits without weight loss | Don't count on it; calorie-matched trials show little benefit from fasting itself |
| Want autophagy | Treat it as a possible bonus, not a goal; human evidence is preliminary and doesn't support long fasts |
| Lift weights | TRE doesn't harm gains if training and protein are in place |
| Have type 2 diabetes | 5:2 can work very well, but only with a medication plan from your doctor |
| Have a history of disordered eating, or are pregnant | Don't fast |

Fasting isn't a metabolic hack, but it's a legitimate, well-studied way to eat less, and for many people the structure is exactly what makes it work.
