Twenty years after a few weeks of computer-based speed training, older adults who had also done two short booster courses were about 25% less likely to have a dementia diagnosis in their Medicare records than people who had done no training at all.

That result, published in February 2026, is one of a small number of randomised findings that link something you can actually do to a lower risk of dementia. Most of what you read about "brain health" rests on far weaker evidence: studies showing that people who eat blueberries, do crosswords or take supplements tend to have better memories, without showing that the blueberries are the cause.

This post focuses on the strongest studies: randomised trials, natural experiments that come close to them, and large meta-analyses. It covers two related goals:

- **Maintaining cognition over the long run**, which mostly means slowing age-related decline and lowering dementia risk.
- **Performing well day to day**, which is about attention, memory and reaction time this week, not in 30 years.

The evidence base for the first goal is much larger, so that's where most of this post goes.

## How to read the evidence

Not all studies carry equal weight. In rough order of trustworthiness:

1. **Randomised trials with hard outcomes.** People are randomly assigned to an intervention or a control, and the outcome is something like a dementia diagnosis. These are rare, because dementia takes decades to develop.
2. **Randomised trials with cognitive test scores.** Much more common. They show whether an intervention changes performance on tests over a few years, which is a reasonable but imperfect stand-in for dementia risk.
3. **Natural experiments.** A policy or an accident assigns people to groups almost at random, for example a vaccine offered only to people born after a certain date. Good ones come close to a trial.
4. **Cohort studies.** Large groups followed over time. Useful for spotting risk factors, but people who exercise, eat well and stay socially active differ from other people in dozens of ways, so cause and effect is hard to untangle.

A recurring theme below: interventions that look spectacular in cohort studies often shrink or vanish in trials. The ones that survive are worth taking seriously.

One more caveat about effect sizes. Many cognitive trials report results in standard deviations (SD). A difference of 0.03 SD on a test battery is real but tiny for any individual. Its value lies in what it might mean when added up across a population and over many years.

## The big picture: 14 modifiable risk factors

The most widely cited summary of dementia prevention is the Lancet Commission on dementia, which updated its report in 2024. It identified 14 risk factors with enough evidence to be considered potentially modifiable:[^1]

- **Early life:** less education.
- **Midlife:** hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, traumatic brain injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, high blood pressure, obesity, excessive alcohol.
- **Later life:** social isolation, air pollution, untreated vision loss.

High LDL cholesterol and vision loss were new in the 2024 report. The Commission estimated that if all 14 were eliminated, about 45% of dementia cases might be prevented or delayed. Hearing loss and high LDL cholesterol had the largest individual shares, about 7% each.

That 45% figure is often quoted as fact, and it shouldn't be. It is a population attributable fraction, a calculation that assumes each risk factor causes dementia and can be fully removed. Neither is fully established for most of the 14. Treat it as a ceiling on what prevention might achieve, and as a map of where to look, not as a promise.

The more useful question is which of these factors have been tested directly in trials. That's where the rest of this post goes.

## Multidomain lifestyle programmes

Because dementia has many contributing causes, several large trials have tested changing many things at once.

### FINGER (Finland, 2015)

The Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study (FINGER) randomised 1,260 people aged 60–77 with elevated dementia risk scores to two years of either general health advice or an intensive programme combining nutritional guidance, supervised exercise, cognitive training and monitoring of vascular risk factors such as blood pressure.[^2]

The intervention group's score on a comprehensive neuropsychological test battery improved slightly more than the control group's, a difference of about 0.02 SD per year. Executive function and processing speed improved more; memory did not differ significantly. FINGER was the first large trial to show that a lifestyle programme could shift cognitive trajectories, and it spawned a worldwide network of similar trials.

### US POINTER (2025)

US POINTER was designed to test whether the FINGER approach works in a more diverse American population. It randomised 2,111 sedentary adults aged 60–79 with a suboptimal diet and additional risk factors, such as a family history of memory problems, to one of two programmes for two years.[^3]

- **Structured:** 38 facilitated peer-group meetings, aerobic exercise 30–35 minutes four times a week plus strength and stretching twice a week, the MIND diet, online cognitive training three times a week, and regular review of blood pressure, weight and lab results with a clinician.
- **Self-guided:** six group meetings, the same general goals, and encouragement without structured coaching.

Global cognition improved in both groups, by about 0.24 SD per year in the structured group and 0.21 SD per year in the self-guided group. The difference, 0.029 SD per year, was statistically significant. The benefit was similar regardless of sex, ethnicity, heart health or APOE ε4 status (the main genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's), and appeared larger in people who started with lower cognitive scores. The structured group also had fewer serious adverse events.

### How much weight can these trials bear?

The direction is encouraging, but there are limits:

- **Small differences.** The between-group effects in both trials were a few hundredths of a standard deviation per year. The trials were not long enough to measure dementia itself.
- **No untreated control in US POINTER.** Both groups changed their lifestyles, so the trial shows that more structure beats less, not how much either beats doing nothing. Part of the improvement in both groups is likely a practice effect from repeating the same tests.
- **Bundled interventions.** You can't tell which component did the work.

A four-year extension of US POINTER is following participants to see whether the gap widens into differences in dementia risk. Until then, the honest summary is: changing several things at once, with support and accountability, produces a modest but real cognitive benefit over two years.

## Blood pressure: the best evidence for a single factor

**SPRINT MIND (2019).** The Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial randomised 9,361 adults aged 50 or older with high blood pressure and elevated cardiovascular risk to a systolic target below 120 mm Hg (intensive) or below 140 mm Hg (standard).[^4] The trial was stopped early, after about 3.3 years of treatment, because the intensive arm sharply reduced heart attacks, strokes and deaths.

For the brain:

- **Probable dementia (the primary outcome):** 7.2 vs 8.6 cases per 1,000 person-years, a 17% lower rate in the intensive group that did not reach statistical significance.
- **Mild cognitive impairment:** 14.6 vs 18.3 cases per 1,000 person-years, a statistically significant 19% reduction.
- **Mild cognitive impairment or dementia combined:** also significantly reduced.

The early stop and fewer dementia cases than expected left the main analysis underpowered. Still, SPRINT MIND showed that aggressive blood pressure lowering is at least safe for the brain, and probably protective. That matters, because older people are sometimes kept at higher blood pressure on the theory that the brain needs it.

The Lancet Commission recommends keeping systolic blood pressure at 130 mm Hg or below from around age 40. If you don't know your blood pressure, measuring it is one of the highest-value things you can do for your brain.

## Hearing: a big signal, in the right people

Hearing loss is the single largest modifiable risk factor in the Lancet estimates, which made the ACHIEVE trial one of the most anticipated in the field.

**ACHIEVE (2023).** 977 adults aged 70–84 with untreated mild-to-moderate hearing loss and no substantial cognitive impairment were randomised to hearing aids plus audiology counselling, or to a health-education control, for three years.[^5]

- **Overall: no effect.** Across the whole trial, the hearing intervention did not slow cognitive decline.
- **High-risk subgroup: 48% slower decline.** Participants were recruited from two sources: an existing heart-health study (ARIC) and new healthy community volunteers. In the ARIC group, who were older, had more risk factors and declined faster, the hearing intervention reduced three-year cognitive decline by 48%. In the healthy volunteers it had no effect.

The subgroup analysis was planned in advance, which makes it more credible than a post hoc finding, but it is still a subgroup. One plausible reading is that healthy volunteers didn't decline enough in three years for any intervention to show a difference.

The practical takeaway is simple: hearing loss is common, treatable and makes social life harder, which may itself matter for cognition. Get your hearing tested, and if you need hearing aids, wear them.

## Exercise: real benefits, smaller than the headlines

Exercise is the most commonly recommended brain-health habit, and the evidence is genuinely mixed in an interesting way.

**The optimistic view.** An umbrella review in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* pooled 133 systematic reviews covering 2,724 randomised trials and more than 250,000 participants of all ages.[^6] Exercise improved general cognition (standardised effect about 0.42), memory (about 0.26) and executive function (about 0.24). Benefits appeared across age groups and health conditions, and even light-intensity exercise helped. Exergames (active video games) and mind-body exercise like tai chi showed some of the largest effects.

**The sceptical view.** An earlier umbrella review in *Nature Human Behaviour*, restricted to healthy people, found a small positive effect (d = 0.22) that dropped to nearly zero (d = 0.05) after correcting for publication bias, the tendency for small positive trials to be published and small negative ones to stay in drawers.[^7]

Both reviews are carefully done; they mostly differ in who they included. The Singh review included people with dementia, ADHD and other conditions, where there's more room to improve and many tests measure impairment. The likely truth: exercise reliably helps people with or at risk of cognitive problems, and the benefit for already-healthy adults on short-term cognitive tests is small.

**Brain structure.** In a frequently cited trial, 120 older adults were randomised to a year of walking three times a week or a stretching-and-toning control.[^8] The walking group's hippocampus, a region central to memory, grew by about 2%, while the control group's shrank by about 1.4%, roughly the loss expected from a year of ageing. Memory improved in both groups, so the structural finding is more convincing than the memory finding.

Exercise also improves blood pressure, blood sugar, mood and sleep, all of which feed into the risk factors above. Even if its direct effect on test scores is modest, it is the intervention that touches the most other factors at once.

## Cognitive training: one kind seems to work

"Brain training" has a poor reputation, and mostly for good reason. A major review of the commercial brain-training industry found that training reliably improves performance on the trained tasks, much less on closely related tasks, and there was little evidence that it improves everyday cognition.[^9] The ACTIVE trial is the notable exception.

**ACTIVE (1998–2019).** The Advanced Cognitive Training for Independent and Vital Elderly trial randomised 2,802 adults aged 65 and older to one of three training programmes or a no-contact control:[^10]

- **Memory training:** mnemonic strategies for remembering lists and text.
- **Reasoning training:** solving problems that follow a pattern, like number series.
- **Speed-of-processing training:** adaptive computer tasks requiring fast identification of objects on screen, including in the periphery, that got harder as performance improved.

Each programme was about 10 sessions over 5–6 weeks. Some participants were later offered booster sessions at roughly one and three years.

Researchers linked 2,021 participants to Medicare records from 1999 to 2019. Over 20 years, 49% of the control group were diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease or a related dementia. Among people who did speed training plus boosters, the figure was 40%, a 25% lower risk (hazard ratio 0.75, 95% CI 0.59–0.95). Speed training without boosters showed no benefit (HR 1.01), and memory and reasoning training did not produce statistically significant reductions.

### How much weight can ACTIVE bear?

This is a striking result from a randomised trial, but independent experts raised fair concerns:

- **Diagnosis from billing records.** Dementia was identified from Medicare claims, not clinical assessment, so it reflects diagnosis rather than underlying disease.
- **A subgroup again.** The benefit appeared only in one of six training-plus-booster combinations. The memory and reasoning arms with boosters also showed lower dementia rates, just not statistically significant ones, which makes "only speed training works" less certain than headlines suggest.
- **Missing data.** People in Medicare Advantage plans were excluded because their claims are incomplete.

Still, it fits an earlier 10-year analysis of the same trial, which also found lower dementia risk after speed training.[^11] An updated version of the speed task is now sold commercially, so it's worth knowing that any general claim that "brain games prevent dementia" goes well beyond this evidence. What ACTIVE supports is narrower: adaptive, speeded visual attention training, repeated over time, may delay a dementia diagnosis.

### What about dual n-back?

Dual n-back is the most famous working-memory training task. You see a square appear in a grid while hearing a letter, and must flag whenever the position, the letter or both match what came *n* steps earlier. As you improve, *n* goes up. It's free, demanding and widely recommended online as a way to raise intelligence.

**The original claim.** Young adults who trained on dual n-back for 8 to 19 days, about 25 minutes a day, improved on a fluid intelligence test (abstract reasoning puzzles), and the gains grew with more training.[^12] Fluid intelligence was thought to be largely fixed, so the paper drew wide attention. But the trained groups were small and the comparison groups did no training at all.

**The replication.** Seventy-five young adults were randomised to 20 sessions of dual n-back, an active control task (adaptive visual search) or no contact.[^13] N-back performance improved a lot. On 17 separate tests of fluid intelligence, multitasking, working memory and perceptual speed, the n-back group did no better than either control group.

**The meta-analyses.** Pooled results depend heavily on one design choice:

- **Au et al. (2015)** pooled 20 n-back studies and found a small improvement in fluid intelligence, equivalent to about 3–4 IQ points.[^14] The effect was mainly found in studies with passive (no-training) control groups. Critics pointed out that small studies showed larger effects, a common sign of publication bias.
- **Melby-Lervåg et al. (2016)** reviewed 87 publications on working-memory training of all kinds.[^15] Training reliably improved performance on similar memory tasks. Studies with active control groups showed essentially no transfer to reasoning, reading or arithmetic. Passive controls know they're in the "nothing" group and have little reason to try hard when retested; active controls do.
- **Soveri et al. (2017)** focused on 33 n-back studies.[^16] Training produced a medium improvement on untrained versions of the n-back task itself, and only small improvements in other working-memory tasks, cognitive control and fluid intelligence.

The consistent finding is that n-back training makes you better at n-back and at very similar tasks. Gains in intelligence or everyday cognition are small at best and largely disappear with proper controls. Almost all of these trials recruited healthy young adults and measured test scores over a few weeks. None measured long-term decline or dementia.

That's the key contrast with ACTIVE, which followed older adults for 20 years and measured diagnoses. If you enjoy dual n-back, it's a harmless mental workout, but it isn't a well-supported use of time for brain health.

### Learning new movement skills: juggling and crawling

Two popular ideas sit between brain training and exercise: learning a demanding motor skill, and unusual movement patterns.

**Juggling.** In a widely cited imaging study, young adults who spent three months learning to juggle showed a temporary increase in grey matter in brain areas that process visual motion.[^17] The increase shrank again after they stopped practising. A follow-up in adults around 60 found similar but smaller changes.[^18] These studies are good evidence that the ageing brain can still change its structure in response to learning. But they measured brain volume and juggling skill, not memory, reasoning or decline, and learning almost any demanding new skill probably does something similar.

**Quadrupedal movement.** Crawling-style training, popularised by programmes like Animal Flow, is often promoted for coordination and the brain. The main study assigned 22 physically active sports science students to four weeks of progressive quadrupedal training or no training.[^19] The trained group improved on a card-sorting test of cognitive flexibility; the control group didn't. With 22 young athletes, a no-training control and test–retest effects, this is pilot-level evidence.

Both are enjoyable, challenging things to learn, and the movement counts as exercise. Neither has evidence of a lasting cognitive benefit.

## Diet: the MIND diet trial was a reality check

The MIND diet combines the Mediterranean and DASH diets, emphasising leafy greens, berries, nuts, whole grains, olive oil and fish. It was developed from cohort data in which people eating this way had markedly slower cognitive decline.

**The MIND diet trial.** 604 adults aged 65–84 with a family history of dementia, a BMI over 25 and a suboptimal diet were randomised to the MIND diet or their usual diet, both with mild calorie restriction (about 250 kcal a day less) and counselling.[^20] After three years:

- Both groups improved on cognitive tests (0.205 SD vs 0.170 SD), and both lost similar amounts of weight.
- There was **no significant difference** in cognition or brain MRI measures between them.

The authors suggest several explanations: the control group also improved its diet, three years may be too short, and weight loss itself may have helped both groups. All plausible. But the cleanest test of the diet's specific effect came back null, and the dramatic cohort associations didn't hold up.

**Blueberries and fibre.** Two specific foods get a lot of attention:

- **Blueberries.** About a dozen small randomised trials have tested blueberry powder or juice, mostly for weeks to a few months. A systematic review found the cognitive results inconsistent: some trials reported better memory or executive function, others nothing, and the tests varied too much to pool.[^21] The trials are small and short, and none measured long-term decline.
- **Prebiotic fibre.** A twin trial randomised 36 pairs of twins aged 60 and over.[^22] Within each pair, one twin took a cheap prebiotic fibre supplement (inulin or fructooligosaccharides) and the other a placebo, for 12 weeks. The fibre group scored better on a memory and learning test often used to detect early Alzheimer's disease, and had more *Bifidobacterium* in their gut. Muscle strength, the main outcome, didn't improve. The twin design controls for genes and upbringing, which is clever, but the trial was small, short and mostly female, and cognition was a secondary outcome. It's an interesting lead, not yet a recommendation.

The practical reading: a healthy diet is clearly good for blood pressure, weight, cholesterol and diabetes risk, which are themselves on the Lancet list. There's no strong evidence that any specific "brain diet" adds much beyond that. MIND was part of the successful US POINTER package, but there it was bundled with everything else.

## Supplements: one modest exception

Most supplements marketed for memory have either not been tested properly or have failed when they were.

**Ginkgo biloba.** The Ginkgo Evaluation of Memory study randomised 3,069 adults aged 75 and over to ginkgo or placebo for a median of about six years.[^23] Ginkgo did not reduce the rate of dementia or Alzheimer's disease.

**Omega-3 fish oil.** Eating fish is linked to better cognition in cohort studies, but capsules have consistently failed in trials. In the VITAL trial, 4,218 adults aged 60 and over took 1 g of marine omega-3 a day or an olive oil placebo for two to three years.[^24] There was no effect on cognitive decline, including in people who started with low omega-3 levels. Two other large trials, AREDS2 (about 3,000 adults over five years) and MAPT (about 1,500 older adults with memory complaints or frailty over three years), also found no overall cognitive benefit.[^25][^26]

**Multivitamins.** The COSMOS trial is the exception worth knowing about. Three separate cognitive substudies, using in-person, phone-based and web-based testing, randomised more than 5,000 older adults to a standard daily multivitamin or placebo for two to three years.[^27] Pooled across the three, the multivitamin group did slightly better on global cognition (0.07 SD) and episodic memory (0.06 SD). The authors translated the global effect into roughly two years less cognitive ageing.

Caveats: the effect is small, all three substudies came from one parent trial, executive function did not improve, and part of the cognition work was funded by a confectionery company's science arm (Mars Edge) with tablets donated by the multivitamin's manufacturer. The same trial found no benefit from cocoa extract on cognition overall.

A standard multivitamin is cheap and low risk, so this is a reasonable thing to consider, particularly if your diet is poor. It is not a substitute for anything else on this list.

**Creatine.** Creatine is best known as a muscle supplement, but the brain also uses it to recycle energy. A 2024 meta-analysis pooled 16 small randomised trials with 492 adults taking creatine monohydrate.[^28] Memory improved slightly (standardised effect 0.31, rated moderate certainty). Effects on attention and processing speed were small and low certainty, and there was no significant effect on overall cognition or executive function. The paper also reports inconsistent study counts in places and has had a published correction.

The more striking result is acute. In a 2024 study, a single very large dose (0.35 g per kg of body weight, about 25 g for a 70 kg person, versus the usual 3–5 g a day) during a night of partial sleep deprivation improved cognitive performance and processing speed, and shifted markers of brain energy metabolism measured with MRI spectroscopy.[^29] Older studies suggest vegetarians, who get little creatine from food, may benefit more.

Like the multivitamin, creatine is cheap and safe at normal doses, with a small and plausible effect. The evidence is too thin to rank it alongside blood pressure, exercise or vaccination.

## Shingles vaccination: the surprise of the decade

**The Welsh natural experiment.** When Wales introduced the shingles vaccine on 1 September 2013, eligibility was set by exact date of birth: people born on or after 2 September 1933 could get it; people born a week earlier never could. Researchers compared the health records of more than 280,000 adults on either side of that cutoff, who were otherwise nearly identical.[^30]

Over seven years, getting the vaccine reduced the probability of a new dementia diagnosis by 3.5 percentage points, a 20% relative reduction among those vaccinated. The vaccine also cut shingles cases by about 37%, close to what its clinical trials found, which suggests the design works. The protective effect appeared stronger in women. The same team has since reported similar signals using records from other countries.

This is a natural experiment rather than a trial, but it is unusually close to one, because the birthday cutoff, not health or lifestyle, decided who could be vaccinated. It is published in *Nature* and is probably the strongest causal evidence in this post apart from SPRINT MIND and ACTIVE.

The Welsh study used Zostavax, an older live vaccine. A separate US records study found that Shingrix, the newer recombinant vaccine used today, was associated with lower dementia risk than Zostavax, though that comparison is observational.[^31] Why a shingles vaccine would protect the brain is not known; reducing viral reactivation and inflammation are leading hypotheses.

If you're eligible for shingles vaccination, the dementia evidence adds a strong reason to get it.

## Sleep: essential for performance, less clear for prevention

Sleep is where the two goals of this post diverge most.

**Day-to-day performance.** In a tightly controlled lab study, 48 healthy adults were restricted to 4, 6 or 8 hours in bed per night for two weeks, or kept awake for three nights.[^32] Restriction to 6 hours a night or less built up cognitive deficits over the two weeks comparable to those from one or two nights of total sleep deprivation. The worrying part: participants' sense of their own sleepiness rose only slightly, so they substantially underestimated how impaired they were.

**Long-term protection.** Poor sleep is linked to dementia in cohort studies, but the relationship runs both ways, since early brain changes disrupt sleep years before diagnosis. There are no large trials showing that improving sleep lowers dementia risk, and the Lancet Commission did not include sleep among its 14 factors.

The practical case for good sleep doesn't depend on settling the dementia question. If your performance this week matters to you, chronically sleeping 6 hours or less is one of the most reliable ways to undermine it, and you probably won't notice.

## Meditation: good for wellbeing, modest for cognition

Meditation is often promoted as a way to keep the brain young. The trials show a smaller, more specific picture.

**Mindfulness and cognition.** A meta-analysis of 45 studies with 2,238 participants found a small benefit for executive function, especially working memory, with slightly larger effects in people over 60.[^33] But the benefit was significant only against inactive control groups. Mindfulness did not outperform active comparisons such as relaxation training, brain training or health education. As with dual n-back, part of the gain may come from expectations and social contact rather than meditation itself.

**The Age-Well trial.** This is the longest and best-designed trial in older adults.[^34] 137 cognitively healthy people aged 65 and over in France were randomised to 18 months of meditation training, a closely matched English-language course, or no intervention. Both courses involved weekly two-hour group sessions plus daily home practice. Meditation did not change the volume of the brain regions it targeted, and differences in blood flow there were not statistically significant. It did outperform language training on a combined measure of attention regulation, emotional and self-awareness capacities.

Meditation has good evidence for reducing stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms, and depression is one of the Lancet risk factors, so there may be an indirect route to brain health. As a direct cognitive intervention, its effects look similar to other engaging, structured activities, and nothing yet links it to lower dementia risk.

## Short-term performance: what works today

For sharper thinking in the short term, the evidence is smaller but consistent for two cheap tools.

- **Caffeine.** A review of military and laboratory research found that doses of roughly 40–300 mg improve vigilance, attention and reaction time, with the biggest benefits when people are sleep deprived.[^35] It's a tool for alertness, not a substitute for sleep, and late doses can make tonight's sleep worse.
- **Caffeine plus L-theanine.** L-theanine, an amino acid found in tea, is often taken with caffeine to smooth out jitters. The review usually cited covers just five small studies, with 5 to 51 participants each and mostly from one research group.[^36] The combination fairly consistently produced faster reaction times and higher alertness ratings; L-theanine on its own had mixed results, including more reported mental fatigue in one study. How much it adds beyond caffeine alone is unclear.
- **A single bout of exercise.** A meta-analysis of 79 studies found that one session of exercise produces a small improvement in cognitive performance afterwards, with the largest effects for moderate intensity and when testing came shortly after exercise.[^37]

## The rest of the list

Several Lancet risk factors have good observational support and obvious health benefits, even without dedicated cognitive trials:

- **Smoking.** Stopping lowers risk at any age.
- **Alcohol.** The Commission flags more than about 21 UK units (roughly 170 g of alcohol) a week as a risk factor.
- **LDL cholesterol.** Newly added in 2024 with support from genetic studies; have it checked from midlife and treat if high.
- **Vision.** Get eyes checked and treat cataracts and other correctable problems.
- **Diabetes and weight.** Both are linked to faster decline.
- **Depression.** Linked to dementia both as a risk factor and an early sign; treatment is worthwhile for its own sake.
- **Social isolation.** Strongly associated with dementia; hard to test in trials, but most multidomain programmes include social activity.
- **Head injury.** Wear helmets for cycling and contact sports, and take concussions seriously.
- **Air pollution.** Mostly a policy issue, but it is part of the list.

## How much weight can this evidence bear?

Pulling the threads together:

- **Strongest causal evidence:** blood pressure control (SPRINT MIND), shingles vaccination (the Welsh natural experiment), speed training with boosters (ACTIVE), and hearing aids for people at higher risk (ACHIEVE subgroup). Each has caveats, and only two measured dementia diagnoses.
- **Consistent but modest:** multidomain lifestyle programmes, exercise, a daily multivitamin and meditation (mainly through mood and stress). Effects on test scores are small; their value is cumulative and long term.
- **Weaker than promised:** specific "brain diets" and foods like blueberries, most supplements including omega-3 fish oil, most commercial brain games and working-memory training such as dual n-back.
- **Too early to tell:** creatine, prebiotic fibre, L-theanine, juggling and quadrupedal movement training, each supported by small or short studies.
- **Mostly observational:** sleep, social engagement, alcohol, smoking and most of the remaining Lancet factors, though several are also good for health in ways that are proven.

Two patterns stand out. First, what's good for your heart is very likely good for your brain; blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar and fitness keep coming up. Second, people who start at higher risk tend to benefit most, which appeared in US POINTER, ACHIEVE and the exercise meta-analyses.

## What this means for you

The table below is a practical reading of the studies above, not a tested prescription. Roughly in order of evidence strength:

| Action | What to do | Based on | Strength of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Control blood pressure | Measure it; aim for ≤130 mm Hg systolic from midlife, with your doctor | SPRINT MIND 2019; Lancet 2024 | Randomised trial |
| Get the shingles vaccine | If eligible, get the current vaccine | Eyting 2025; Taquet 2024 | Strong natural experiment |
| Treat hearing loss | Get a hearing test; wear hearing aids if needed | ACHIEVE 2023 | Trial subgroup |
| Exercise regularly | Mix of aerobic and strength work; any intensity helps, more structure may help more | Singh 2025; US POINTER 2025 | Many trials, modest effects |
| Combine habits, with support | Group, coach or programme with goals and regular check-ins | FINGER 2015; US POINTER 2025 | Randomised trials, small effects |
| Speed-of-processing training | Adaptive visual speed tasks, with periodic refreshers | ACTIVE 2026 | One long trial, debated |
| Consider a multivitamin | Standard daily multivitamin, especially with a poor diet | COSMOS 2024 | Trial, small effect |
| Protect sleep | Avoid chronic 6-hour nights, especially before demanding work | Van Dongen 2003 | Strong for performance only |
| Check the other risk factors | Stop smoking; moderate alcohol; check LDL, eyes and blood sugar; stay socially connected | Lancet 2024 | Mostly observational |

If you notice memory changes that worry you, talk to your doctor rather than reaching for a supplement or app. Several causes of cognitive symptoms, including thyroid problems, low vitamin B12, depression, sleep apnoea and medication side effects, are treatable.
