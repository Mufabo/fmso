Meditation is sold as everything from a stress reliever to a brain upgrade. The research is more modest, but also more solid than the sceptics suggest. After a few hundred randomized trials, the picture is fairly clear: **regular meditation produces small to moderate improvements in anxiety, depression and stress, and in some conditions it works about as well as standard treatments. It is not better than other active treatments, it doesn't work for everyone, and it isn't risk-free.**

This post covers how to start, then what the best studies show, how much practice you actually need, and what to watch out for.

*This post is general information, not medical advice. If you have a diagnosed mental health condition, especially PTSD, psychosis or bipolar disorder, talk to your clinician before starting an intensive practice.*

## What "meditation" means

"Meditation" covers several quite different practices. Most of the research uses one of these:

| Type | What you do | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Focused attention** | Rest attention on one object (usually the breath) and bring it back each time it wanders | Breath counting, basic mindfulness of breathing |
| **Open monitoring** | Notice whatever arises (sounds, sensations, thoughts) without following it | "Choiceless awareness", noting practice |
| **Body scan** | Move attention slowly through the body | Core MBSR practice |
| **Loving-kindness / compassion** | Silently repeat well-wishing phrases for yourself and others | Metta |
| **Mantra** | Repeat a word or sound | Transcendental Meditation |

Most of the trials below test **mindfulness-based programmes**, especially:

- **MBSR (Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction):** an 8-week group course with weekly 2–2.5-hour sessions, a day-long retreat, and daily home practice. It mixes body scan, sitting meditation and gentle yoga.
- **MBCT (Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy):** the same structure, combined with elements of cognitive therapy, designed to prevent relapse in recurrent depression.

## How to start

### A first session (10 minutes)

1. **Sit comfortably.** A chair is fine. Keep your back reasonably upright but not rigid, hands resting on your thighs. Eyes closed or softly looking at the floor.
2. **Set a timer** so you're not checking the clock.
3. **Find the breath.** Pick one spot where you feel it most clearly: the nostrils, chest, or belly.
4. **Count if it helps.** Count each out-breath from 1 to 10, then start again.
5. **Notice when you've wandered.** You will, within seconds. When you realise you're thinking about something else, note "thinking" and return to the breath.
6. **Repeat step 5 a hundred times.** That return *is* the practice. A session where you wandered off 50 times and came back 50 times was not a failed session.

### Common beginner problems

- **"I can't stop thinking."** Nobody can. The goal isn't an empty mind; it's noticing sooner that you've drifted.
- **Sleepiness.** Sit more upright, open your eyes, or practise earlier in the day.
- **Restlessness or discomfort.** Shift position mindfully. Walking meditation is a legitimate alternative.
- **Strong emotions or unpleasant memories coming up.** This happens and is covered in the risks section below. Opening your eyes, shortening sessions, or focusing on external sounds instead of the breath all help.

### Apps, courses or teachers?

- **Apps** are the easiest entry point and do work, though with small effects (see below).
- **An 8-week MBSR or MBCT course** is what most of the strongest evidence is based on. The group structure and a teacher make it far easier to keep practising.
- **For depression relapse prevention specifically**, MBCT is the version with the evidence.

## What the research shows

### The big picture: stress, anxiety and depression

The most cited summary is a 2014 systematic review and meta-analysis in *JAMA Internal Medicine* by Madhav Goyal and colleagues at Johns Hopkins. It pooled 47 randomized trials with more than 3,500 participants with a range of conditions, from anxiety and depression to chronic pain, heart disease and cancer[^1]. It found:

- **Moderate-strength evidence** that mindfulness programmes improve anxiety and depression, with low or insufficient evidence for most other outcomes[^1].
- Meditation programmes produced **small to moderate reductions** in multiple negative dimensions of psychological stress[^1].
- **No evidence that meditation was better than other active treatments**, such as drugs, exercise or other behavioural therapies[^1].
- Low evidence of no effect, or insufficient evidence, for mantra-based programmes[^2].

A larger 2021 meta-analysis in *PLOS Medicine* by Julieta Galante's team at Cambridge looked at people *without* a diagnosis, which is most people who meditate. It included 136 randomized trials and 11,605 participants from 29 countries[^3]. Compared with doing nothing, mindfulness courses reduced anxiety, depression and distress and improved well-being. But the prediction intervals crossed zero, meaning that in some settings mindfulness programmes may not improve these outcomes at all[^3]. Compared with other active well-being practices, mindfulness was mostly not clearly better; the exception was depression against nonspecific controls[^4].

**Takeaway:** meditation is a real, if modest, intervention. Think of it as one good option among several (exercise, therapy, social connection), not a superior one.

### Anxiety disorders: as good as an antidepressant?

The most striking recent trial is TAME, published in *JAMA Psychiatry* in 2023 by Elizabeth Hoge and colleagues. It randomized 276 adults with diagnosed anxiety disorders at three US academic medical centres to either an 8-week MBSR course or the antidepressant escitalopram (10–20 mg); 208 completed the trial[^5].

On a 7-point clinician-rated severity scale, symptoms fell by 1.35 points with MBSR and 1.43 with escitalopram, a statistically equivalent result, so MBSR was judged noninferior[^6]. MBSR was also associated with fewer adverse events[^7].

Caveats worth knowing: participants couldn't be blinded to which treatment they got, and the MBSR group had much more contact time with clinicians. And as Hoge herself noted, mindfulness only works if you invest the time to attend sessions and practise at home[^6].

### Preventing depression from coming back

This is where the evidence is strongest. In 2016, Willem Kuyken and colleagues published an individual patient data meta-analysis in *JAMA Psychiatry*, pooling raw data from nine randomized trials of MBCT for people with recurrent depression in full or partial remission (1,258 patients with data)[^8]. Over 60 weeks of follow-up:

- People who received MBCT had a lower risk of depressive relapse than those who didn't, and comparisons with active treatments, including maintenance antidepressants, also favoured MBCT[^8][^9].
- Age, sex, education and age at first depression didn't significantly change how well MBCT worked[^9].

MBCT is recommended in UK NICE guidelines for this purpose. Note what it is: an 8-week therapist-led programme for people who have had depression before, not a meditation app.

### Chronic pain

The best single trial is Daniel Cherkin's 2016 study in *JAMA*. It randomized 342 adults aged 20 to 70 with chronic low back pain to MBSR, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) or usual care, with 8 weekly 2-hour group sessions[^10]. Both MBSR and CBT led to greater improvement in pain and functional limitation than usual care at 26 and 52 weeks[^11]. Among those trained in mindfulness, 43.6% reported a meaningful reduction in pain[^12].

MBSR wasn't better than CBT, but it was as good, which matters because access to CBT for pain is limited.

### Sleep

In a 2015 *JAMA Internal Medicine* trial, David Black and colleagues randomized 49 older adults with moderate sleep problems to a 6-week mindfulness course (2 hours a week) or a sleep hygiene education course[^13]. The mindfulness group reported better sleep quality than the sleep education group[^14], with improvements carrying over into less daytime fatigue and depressive symptoms[^15].

It's a small trial in older adults with moderate (not clinical) sleep problems. For diagnosed insomnia, CBT for insomnia (CBT-I) remains the first-line treatment.

### Stress physiology: cortisol, blood pressure, heart rate

A frequent criticism of meditation studies is that they compare meditators with people doing nothing. Michaela Pascoe and colleagues addressed that in a 2017 meta-analysis that included only trials against an **active** control group: 45 studies in total[^16]. Meditation reduced cortisol, systolic blood pressure and heart rate compared with these active controls[^17].

### The immune system and inflammation

Chronic stress raises low-grade inflammation and can blunt immune responses, so it's plausible that a stress-reducing practice would show up in immune markers. The evidence says it does, a little, but "meditation boosts your immune system" overstates it.

**Inflammation markers.** The first systematic review of randomized trials on this, by David Black and George Slavich in 2016, looked at five kinds of outcomes: inflammatory proteins, inflammatory gene expression, immune cell counts, immune cell ageing, and antibody responses[^39]. Studies differed so much in populations, designs and lab methods that the authors could only conclude meditation *may* be beneficial for the immune system, pending more work[^39]. A 2022 meta-analysis by Thomas Dunn and Mirena Dimolareva quantified it, pooling 48 trials with 4,683 participants[^40]:

| Marker | What it is | Effect of mindfulness programmes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C-reactive protein (CRP) | General inflammation marker | Small reduction after the programme (−0.14), larger at follow-up (−0.39)[^40] |
| Interleukin-6 (IL-6) | Pro-inflammatory cytokine | Reduction after the programme (−0.35), not significant at follow-up[^40] |
| CD4+ T cells | Immune cells (relevant in HIV) | No significant change[^40] |
| Telomerase activity | Enzyme that maintains chromosome ends | Increase (+0.81), but with a very wide confidence interval[^40] |
| Telomere length | Marker of cellular ageing | Borderline increase (+0.12)[^40] |

A 2023 network meta-analysis comparing psychological interventions found mindfulness-based programmes reduced pro-inflammatory markers by a moderate amount (d = −0.38), alongside other approaches such as CBT[^41]. So the anti-inflammatory effect seems real but modest, and probably not unique to meditation: it likely comes from reducing stress, which other effective interventions also do.

**Vaccine responses.** A widely publicised 2003 study by Richard Davidson and Jon Kabat-Zinn vaccinated 41 employees against influenza after 25 of them had done 8 weeks of mindfulness training; the meditators produced more antibodies than the waitlist group[^42]. It was small, though, and a later, larger trial in adults aged 50 and over found no difference in vaccine responses between MBSR, exercise and control groups[^43]. What did predict a stronger antibody response was being less anxious, less stressed and more optimistic, whatever group people were in[^43].

**Colds and flu.** Bruce Barrett's group at the University of Wisconsin ran two trials of meditation or exercise to prevent acute respiratory infections. The first, in 154 adults aged 50 and over, found markedly lower illness severity after MBSR than in controls, and 16 days of work missed in the meditation group versus 67 in the control group[^44]. The second, larger trial randomized 413 adults[^45]. Meditation reduced the number of people getting sick, but the result (p = 0.036) didn't meet the trial's pre-set significance threshold, and the authors noted the trial was likely underpowered[^46].

**Takeaway:** meditation produces small reductions in inflammation markers, especially CRP and IL-6, and may modestly reduce the burden of colds. It hasn't been shown to make you more resistant to infection in a clinically reliable way, and it doesn't replace vaccination, sleep or exercise, which have far stronger evidence for immune health.

### Attention and memory in complete beginners

What about short, everyday practice? Julia Basso and Wendy Suzuki's team at NYU randomized 42 people aged 18 to 45 with no meditation experience to either 13 minutes of daily guided meditation or 13 minutes of daily podcast listening for 8 weeks[^18][^19]. After 8 weeks, but not after 4, the meditation group had less anxiety and negative mood and better attention and memory, and lower anxiety during a lab stress test[^20].

That makes it one of the more encouraging studies for beginners: about a quarter-hour a day was enough. It is also a single, small trial, so treat it as promising rather than settled.

### Does meditation change your brain?

You've probably seen claims that eight weeks of mindfulness thickens your cortex or shrinks your amygdala. These came from a few small studies that have been cited thousands of times despite major methodological limitations[^21].

In 2022, Tammi Kral, Richard Davidson and colleagues at the University of Wisconsin tested this properly. They pooled two randomized trials with 218 healthy meditation-naive adults, comparing MBSR with an active control programme (the Health Enhancement Program, similar to MBSR but without mindfulness) and a waitlist, using MRI before and after[^22][^23]. They found **no evidence that two months of mindfulness training produced structural brain changes**[^21], and no differences between MBSR and either control group[^24].

That doesn't mean meditation has no brain effects. Functional changes, or structural changes after years of practice, are still open questions. But the "8 weeks rewires your brain" headline doesn't hold up.

### Apps

Most beginners start with an app, so this matters. A 2024 meta-analysis in *Clinical Psychology Review* pooled 43 randomized trials of mindfulness apps[^25]. Apps had small but significant effects on depression (g = 0.24) and anxiety (g = 0.28), and weren't significantly different from active therapeutic comparisons, though few such trials existed[^26]. An earlier meta-analysis of 34 trials found a somewhat larger effect on perceived stress (g = 0.46)[^27].

In plain terms: an app helps a bit, on average, and the effect is smaller than a full course. The authors note that higher-quality studies with longer follow-up are still needed[^25].

## What doesn't hold up

### Mindfulness for everyone, everywhere

The MYRIAD trial is the largest test of mindfulness as a universal intervention. It randomized 85 UK secondary schools and about 8,400 students aged 11–14 to a school mindfulness curriculum or normal social-emotional teaching[^28]. The findings did not show that mindfulness training was better than usual teaching for students' mental health[^29], and follow-up analyses concluded the curriculum as delivered wasn't indicated for reducing risk of mental ill health[^30].

It's a useful reminder: meditation helps people who actually practise it and want to, and doesn't automatically scale to a whole population.

### The hype problem

In 2018, fifteen meditation researchers published "Mind the Hype" in *Perspectives on Psychological Science*[^31]. Their argument: mindfulness has moved from fringe topic to corporate well-being tool and therapy replacement faster than the evidence justifies. Common problems include vague definitions of "mindfulness", small samples, and many studies lacking an active control group[^32]. The lead author, Nicholas Van Dam, stressed the authors believed meditation might help people, but that the rigour wasn't there yet[^32].

The research has improved since then (TAME, Kral, Galante and MYRIAD are exactly the kind of studies they called for), and the better studies have mostly confirmed modest benefits while puncturing the bigger claims.

## How much should you practise?

There's no proven minimum, but the evidence gives a reasonable range:

- **About 10–15 minutes a day for 8 weeks** produced measurable benefits in beginners in Basso's trial, and 4 weeks wasn't enough[^20].
- **Standard MBSR and MBCT courses ask for about 45 minutes a day, six days a week**, plus weekly group sessions[^34].
- **People actually do about two-thirds of that.** A 2017 meta-analysis of 43 studies (1,427 participants) found participants completed 64% of assigned home practice, about 30 minutes a day, six days a week[^33].
- **More practice helps, but only a little.** The same meta-analysis found a small but significant link between the amount of formal practice and better outcomes[^35].

**In practice:** consistency matters more than length. Ten minutes every day beats an hour on Sunday. Give it at least 8 weeks before judging whether it works for you.

## Risks and adverse effects

Meditation is usually presented as harmless. It mostly is, but not always.

In 2020, Miguel Farias and colleagues published the first systematic review of meditation adverse events in *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*. It covered 83 studies with 6,703 participants[^36]. The overall prevalence of adverse events was 8.3%, ranging from 3.7% in experimental studies to 33.2% in observational ones[^37]. The most common were anxiety and depression, and adverse effects also occurred in people with no previous history of mental health problems[^38].

Reported problems range from mild and temporary (agitation, unpleasant memories, sleep disturbance) to rarer serious ones such as depersonalisation, panic or, in very rare cases with intensive retreats, psychotic episodes.

**Practical precautions:**

- Start with short sessions (5–15 minutes); avoid jumping straight into long silent retreats.
- If you have a history of trauma, PTSD, psychosis or bipolar disorder, work with a teacher or clinician who knows trauma-sensitive approaches.
- If practice reliably makes you feel worse, stop or change technique. Open-eyed practice, walking meditation, or focusing on sounds can be gentler than breath focus for some people.
- Meditation can complement treatment for anxiety or depression, but don't stop medication without talking to your doctor.

## An 8-week beginner plan

| Week | Daily practice | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 5 min | Breath counting, 1 to 10. Just build the habit |
| 2 | 10 min | Breath focus without counting; note "thinking" and return |
| 3 | 10 min | Alternate days: body scan (guided recording) |
| 4 | 10–15 min | Add 2–3 short "breathing spaces" (1–3 min) during the day |
| 5 | 15 min | Open awareness: sounds, sensations, thoughts as they arise |
| 6 | 15 min | Try loving-kindness once or twice this week |
| 7 | 15–20 min | Pick the practice that suits you best |
| 8 | 15–20 min | Review: are you calmer, sleeping better, less reactive? |

If you want the structure most of the evidence is based on, a local or online MBSR course covers the same ground with a teacher and group.

## Bottom line

| If you want... | The evidence says |
| --- | --- |
| Less stress and anxiety | Small to moderate benefit; comparable to other active approaches, not better[^1][^3] |
| Treatment for an anxiety disorder | MBSR performed about as well as escitalopram in one large trial[^6] |
| To prevent depression coming back | MBCT has the strongest evidence of any meditation programme[^8] |
| Help with chronic back pain | MBSR about as effective as CBT and better than usual care[^11] |
| Better sleep | Promising in older adults; CBT-I is still first-line for insomnia[^14] |
| A stronger immune system | Small reductions in inflammation markers; vaccine and cold/flu results are mixed[^40][^43][^46] |
| Sharper attention | Early evidence from ~13 min/day for 8 weeks[^20] |
| A "rewired" brain in 8 weeks | Not supported by the largest controlled study[^21] |
| A zero-risk practice | Adverse effects occur in a minority; start gently[^37] |

Meditation isn't a miracle, but it's one of the better-studied, low-cost, self-directed tools for mental health. Ten to fifteen minutes a day for two months is a fair experiment.
