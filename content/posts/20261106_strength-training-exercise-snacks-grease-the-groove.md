In a 2022 study, young adults did one maximal lowering contraction of the biceps a day, five days a week. Each rep took three seconds. After four weeks, the total training time was one minute, and their arm strength had gone up by more than 10% (Sato et al., 2022)[^11].

A follow-up was even more striking. Six of those contractions a day, five days a week, made people stronger. The same 30 contractions crammed into one session per week did not (Yoshida et al., 2022)[^15].

That is the core question of this post: can you get meaningfully stronger with nothing but short, frequent bouts spread through the day, and no workouts at all? Strength coaches have a name for one version of this, "greasing the groove." Exercise scientists call another version resistance exercise snacks. The two ideas come from different worlds but are converging on the same answer.

For the cardio side of exercise snacks (stair sprints, VO2max and the UK Biobank data), see my earlier post on [exercise snacks]({{< relref "20261031_exercise-snacks.md" >}}). This one is only about strength.

## Two traditions, one idea

**Greasing the groove (GTG).** Pavel Tsatsouline popularised the term in the late 1990s and in his book *The Naked Warrior* (Tsatsouline, 2003)[^14]. The recipe is simple:

- pick one or two movements you want to get better at, such as pull-ups or push-ups;
- do many sets spread across the day, often every hour or so;
- keep every set well short of failure, roughly half of what you could do fresh;
- do this most days of the week.

The rationale is that strength is partly a skill. Practising a movement often, while fresh, teaches your nervous system to recruit and coordinate the right muscles more efficiently. Pavel credited the frequency principle to Soviet coaching and to Vladimir Zatsiorsky's advice to train as often as possible while staying as fresh as possible.

**Resistance exercise snacks.** In the research literature, exercise snacks started as a cardio tool: short bursts of vigorous effort, several times a day. More recently, researchers have tested strength versions: a few minutes of squats, lunges, push-ups or calf raises, repeated once to many times a day, at home or at work.

The difference is mostly about effort. GTG deliberately stays easy. Most snack trials push each bout fairly hard. Both rely on the same lever: frequency.

## Why frequent, short bouts could build strength

Strength gains in the first weeks of training come mostly from the nervous system, not from bigger muscles. Classic reviews by Sale (1988)[^10] and Folland and Williams (2007)[^2] describe the main changes: more motor units recruited, faster firing rates and better coordination between muscles that help and muscles that oppose a movement. Muscle growth contributes more over months.

If early strength is largely neural learning, then practising more often should help, much like piano practice works better as daily short sessions than one long weekly marathon. That is the theory. Here is what the studies show.

## Evidence 1: training frequency in conventional programs

The broadest evidence comes from meta-analyses of ordinary gym programmes that compared training a muscle once, twice, three or more times a week.

**Grgic et al. (2018), *Sports Medicine*[^4].** Across 22 studies, strength gains rose with frequency. Effect sizes were 0.74, 0.82, 0.93 and 1.08 for training 1, 2, 3 and 4 or more times a week. But most high-frequency groups also did more total work. When the analysis was limited to studies that matched weekly volume, frequency no longer had a significant effect. The authors' practical reading: higher frequency is a convenient way to fit in more total volume, and it may matter more for multi-joint lifts, which have a bigger skill component.

**Ralston et al. (2018), *Sports Medicine – Open*[^8].** A separate meta-analysis of 12 studies reached a similar conclusion. When volume was equated, training once a week and three or more times a week produced similar strength gains, with only a non-significant trend favouring higher frequency.

**Splitting the day's work in two (Häkkinen & Kallinen, 1994)[^5].** Ten trained female athletes did three weeks of normal strength training, then three weeks in which the same daily volume was split into two sessions. Maximal strength and muscle cross-sectional area increased significantly only in the split period, and individual strength gains tracked changes in muscle activation. It is a tiny, uncontrolled study, but it is one of the few to test splitting a fixed daily dose.

**Takeaway.** In conventional training, frequency is mostly a tool for accumulating volume. On its own, it gives at best a small extra benefit. That sounds discouraging for snacks, but these studies tested 1 to 6 sessions a week, not 20 to 50 tiny ones. The next set of studies looks at what happens at the extreme.

## Evidence 2: micro-doses of eccentric training

Ken Nosaka (Edith Cowan University, Australia) and Masatoshi Nakamura (Niigata University of Health and Welfare, Japan) ran a series of studies that are the closest thing in the literature to a lab test of GTG-style frequency. All used maximal contractions of the elbow flexors on an isokinetic machine in young, untrained adults, over four weeks.

**One 3-second contraction a day (Sato et al., 2022)[^11].** Participants did a single maximal isometric, concentric (lifting) or eccentric (lowering) contraction once a day, five days a week: 20 contractions, one minute of total effort. A control group did nothing.

- The eccentric group improved isometric strength by about 10%, concentric by 13% and eccentric by 12%.
- The concentric group improved only isometric strength (about 6%).
- The isometric group improved only eccentric strength (about 7%).
- Muscle thickness did not change in any group.

**Daily vs weekly, same volume (Yoshida et al., 2022)[^15].** Three groups did maximal eccentric contractions for four weeks:

| Group | Schedule | Contractions per week | Strength | Muscle thickness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 × 5 | 6 contractions a day, 5 days a week | 30 | +9% to +14% across all three tests | +10% |
| 30 × 1 | 30 contractions in one weekly session | 30 | No significant change | +8% |
| 6 × 1 | 6 contractions in one weekly session | 6 | No change | No change |

Same weekly volume, very different outcome for strength. Muscle size, on the other hand, responded to volume regardless of how it was distributed. The daily group's strength gains were similar to the one-contraction-a-day group in the earlier study, so piling on more daily reps did not add much strength in four weeks.

**How few days are enough? (Yoshida et al., 2024)[^16].** The same single 3-second eccentric contraction done twice a week produced no significant gains. Three times a week produced small gains in concentric (about 2.5%) and eccentric strength (about 4%), clearly less than five days a week. The authors concluded that at least three days a week were needed for this tiny dose, and that more frequent sessions appeared to work better.

**What to make of this.** These are small studies (12–13 per group) on one muscle group, using a lab machine that can deliver a true maximal eccentric effort. They do not tell you that a single negative pull-up a day will transform your back. But they are the cleanest evidence that, for strength specifically, how often you train can matter more than how much you do per session, and that lowering contractions give the most strength per second of effort.

## Evidence 3: one squat session or three squat snacks?

**Fragmented vs traditional training (Zhai et al., 2026, *Journal of Exercise Science & Fitness*)[^17].** This trial asked the most practical version of the question directly. Forty-five recreationally trained young men were randomised to:

- **fragmented training:** three short bouts a day of 20 Smith-machine squats, at least an hour apart (morning, afternoon, evening), each with a brief warm-up;
- **traditional training:** 3 sets of 20 squats in one session, with 3 minutes' rest between sets;
- **control:** usual activity.

Both groups trained three days a week for eight weeks, with load progressing from 40% to 55% of their one-rep max. Both improved 1RM, isometric squat force and muscular endurance compared with the control group, with no statistically significant difference between them. The traditional group did slightly better on paper, for example about 14 extra endurance reps over control versus about 10 for the fragmented group, but the trial was not large enough to tell whether that gap is real. The fragmented group enjoyed its training more.

The limits matter: one exercise, moderate loads, young men, eight weeks. Still, it is a randomised, volume-matched comparison, and it suggests that chopping a session into snacks costs you little, if anything.

## Evidence 4: bodyweight snack programmes

These trials are closest to what most people would actually do: no equipment, at home or at work.

**Sedentary young women, 75 minutes a week (Shang et al., 2026, *Frontiers in Physiology*)[^13].** Sixty women aged 18–25 who sat at least 8 hours a day were randomised to an app-guided snack programme or to their usual routine for eight weeks. The snack group did 1-minute bouts of squats, jumping jacks, wall sits, squat jumps and lunges, 30 minutes apart, 10–11 times a day, every day, at an effort of at least 7 out of 10. The app checked form and logged each bout; adherence was reported as 100%.

| Outcome | Snack group | Control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knee extension peak torque | +12.3% | +2.5% |
| Isometric mid-thigh pull | +2.1% | −0.2% |
| Standing long jump | +4.3% | +0.7% |
| Bodyweight squats to failure | +17.9% | +9.3% |

All differences between groups were statistically significant. Note the pattern: large gains in single-joint knee strength and endurance, but only a small change in whole-body pulling force. Bodyweight leg work trains the legs; it does not do much for a deadlift-type pull. And 10–11 bouts a day is a lot of interruptions.

**Office workers, 10 minutes a workday (Brandt et al., 2024, *Frontiers in Public Health*)[^1].** Women with desk jobs who did little or no resistance training did 10 minutes of bodyweight exercise snacks during working hours, five days a week, for 12 weeks. The snack group gained 0.42 kg of muscle mass while controls lost 0.16 kg. Strength did not differ significantly between groups, although trunk and upper-body push strength trended in the snack group's favour. Participants chose their own group rather than being randomised, so treat this as a pilot.

**Older adults.** The Bath pilot (Perkin et al., 2019)[^7] had healthy older adults do two 5-minute snacks a day for 28 days and found better sit-to-stand performance (+31%), leg power and thigh muscle size. An Australian trial (Fyfe et al., 2022)[^3] randomised older adults to 9-minute snacks once, twice or three times a day for four weeks. Adherence was 97%, 82% and 81% respectively, which is a useful reality check: more snacks per day means more missed snacks. The 2026 *British Journal of Sports Medicine* meta-analysis found that snacks improved muscular endurance in older adults but not maximal strength (Rodríguez et al., 2026)[^9]. My [exercise snacks]({{< relref "20261031_exercise-snacks.md" >}}) post covers these trials in more detail.

**Bodyweight progressions can match weights.** A related, non-snack study is useful here. Moderately trained men who did progressively harder push-up variations three times a week for four weeks improved their bench press 1RM similarly to men who trained on the bench press itself (Kotarsky et al., 2018)[^6]. Bodyweight work can build real strength, but only if the exercise keeps getting harder.

## What about greasing the groove itself?

I could not find a peer-reviewed trial that tests GTG as Pavel describes it: many easy, submaximal sets of one movement through the day, compared against a normal programme. Some blog posts cite such studies, but I couldn't verify them. GTG's support is indirect:

- **Supported:** early strength gains are largely neural (Sale, 1988[^10]; Folland & Williams, 2007[^2]); high frequency allows lots of practice without much fatigue (Grgic et al., 2018)[^4]; spreading work out can beat cramming it, at least for very low volumes (Yoshida et al., 2022[^15]; Yoshida et al., 2024[^16]); splitting a session into bouts doesn't lose much (Zhai et al., 2026)[^17].
- **Not tested:** whether sets at around 50% of maximum, never close to failure, build as much strength as harder snacks; how long progress continues before it stalls; whether gains in, say, pull-up reps transfer to other strength measures.

GTG is a plausible, well-grounded method, not a proven one. The strongest positive snack trials used harder efforts than classic GTG prescribes.

## What snacks-only training probably won't do

- **Build much muscle.** In the Nosaka/Nakamura studies, muscle size followed total volume, not frequency. A meta-analysis by Schoenfeld, Grgic and Krieger (2019)[^12] found the same for hypertrophy in general: when weekly volume is matched, frequency doesn't matter much. Snacks can still help if they add volume you wouldn't otherwise do, but easy GTG sets add little growth stimulus.
- **Give broad strength.** Gains are specific. Squat snacks make you better at squats and leg extension, not at pulling a heavy weight off the floor (Shang et al., 2026)[^13].
- **Progress forever with bodyweight.** Air squats stop being a strength stimulus quickly. Without harder variations or added load, gains will plateau.
- **Replace heavy training for advanced lifters.** Nearly all positive trials used untrained or recreationally trained people over 4–12 weeks.

## A snacks-only strength routine

This is my reading of the studies above, not a tested programme. It combines GTG's easy practice with the harder efforts that worked in the snack trials.

**Pick 3–4 movements** that together cover the body:

| Pattern | Easier → harder |
| --- | --- |
| Push | Incline push-up → push-up → deficit or archer push-up |
| Squat | Chair sit-to-stand → split squat → rear-foot-elevated split squat → pistol to a box |
| Pull | Table or ring row → negative pull-up → pull-up |
| Hinge / posterior chain | Glute bridge → single-leg bridge → single-leg Romanian deadlift (with a bag or kettlebell) |

**Two kinds of snack.**

1. **Groove snacks (GTG).** One set at about half your current max reps, crisp and fast, no grinding. Several times a day, at least 15–30 minutes apart (most snack studies used an hour or more). Good for the movement you most want to improve, such as pull-ups.
2. **Hard snacks.** One or two sets at an effort of about 7–8 out of 10, or a single slow negative (3–5 seconds lowering) of a variation you can't yet do for reps. Once or twice a day per movement. This mirrors the effort in the Shang trial[^13] and the eccentric focus of the Nosaka work[^11].

**Weekly structure.**

- At least 3 days a week per movement, ideally 5. Two days a week did nothing in the micro-dose eccentric studies[^16].
- Rotate: for example, push and squat in the morning, pull and hinge in the afternoon.
- Total time: typically 5–15 minutes a day, spread across 4–10 snacks.

**Progression.**

- Retest your max reps (or hold time) every 1–2 weeks and reset the GTG set to half the new number.
- When you can do about 12–15 clean reps of a variation, move to the next harder one.
- If performance drops for several days or joints get achy, take 3–4 easy days. GTG's main risk is overuse, because the same joint gets loaded many times a day.

**Make it stick.** Tie snacks to existing habits: a push-up set before each coffee, a few split squats when you stand up from a meeting, a negative pull-up every time you walk past the bar. The Fyfe trial[^3] suggests a realistic number of daily snacks you'll actually keep doing is better than an ambitious one you'll skip.

## Bottom line

- **You can get stronger with snacks only**, especially if you are currently untrained. Randomised trials show gains in leg strength, endurance and power from bodyweight snacks (Shang et al., 2026)[^13], and splitting a squat session into three bouts worked about as well as one session (Zhai et al., 2026)[^17].
- **Frequency matters most at very low volumes.** Tiny daily doses beat the same work crammed into one weekly session for strength (Yoshida et al., 2022)[^15], and 3+ days a week seems to be the minimum (Yoshida et al., 2024)[^16].
- **Eccentric emphasis gives the most strength per second.** Slow, hard lowering phases are an efficient snack.
- **Greasing the groove is plausible but untested as such.** It is well suited to improving rep maxes in specific movements, less so to building muscle.
- **Progression is non-negotiable.** Without harder variations or load, snack-only training stalls.

If you already lift, snacks are a good way to add practice and volume on top. If you don't, they are a legitimate, evidence-backed way to start.
