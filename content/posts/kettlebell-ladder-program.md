Three workouts a week, one kettlebell, and three simple pairings: goblet squats, leg raises and push-ups, each alternated with swings in a descending 4-3-2-1 ladder. Every workout has ten ladders, and every pairing gets exactly ten ladders across the week.

For most beginners that means a 12 kg bell for men who have never trained (16 kg if you have some training behind you) and an 8 kg bell for women (12 kg with some training), rest of about 2 minutes between ladders at first, shrinking toward 60 seconds over the following weeks. The rest of this post explains how the program is put together, how to pick the bell and the rest periods, how to progress, and what the research says this kind of training can and cannot do.

*This post is general information, not medical advice. If you have a heart condition, back problems or have not exercised in a long time, check with your doctor first, and get someone qualified to look at your swing.*

## The program at a glance

**One ladder** pairs a strength exercise with swings, counting down from 4:

> 4 goblet squats → 4 swings → 3 squats → 3 swings → 2 squats → 2 swings → 1 squat → 1 swing

That is 10 reps of the strength exercise and 10 swings, and it takes roughly a minute to a minute and a half.

**One workout** is 10 ladders, split 5 / 3 / 2 between the three pairings. The split rotates so that each pairing gets the big block once a week:

| Pairing | Day A (Mon) | Day B (Wed) | Day C (Fri) | Weekly ladders |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Goblet squat + swing | 5 | 2 | 3 | 10 |
| Leg raise + swing | 3 | 5 | 2 | 10 |
| Push-up + swing | 2 | 3 | 5 | 10 |
| **Ladders per workout** | **10** | **10** | **10** | **30** |

Do the pairings in blocks, starting with the one that has 5 ladders while you are freshest. Train on three non-consecutive days.

**Weekly volume** with full 4-3-2-1 ladders:

| Exercise | Per ladder | Per workout | Per week |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Swings | 10 | 100 | 300 |
| Goblet squats | 10 | 50, 20 or 30 | 100 |
| Leg raises | 10 | 30, 50 or 20 | 100 |
| Push-ups | 10 | 20, 30 or 50 | 100 |

The ladder format does two jobs at once. The swings keep your heart rate up between strength reps, which is the endurance side. The strength exercise gets many short, crisp sets with a little fatigue building each time, which is the muscle side. And because the reps count down, every ladder ends on the easiest round, which makes it easy to keep form clean.

## Choosing the kettlebell

Pick the heaviest bell you can swing with a crisp hip snap *and* goblet squat for about 8 controlled reps. With only one bell, the goblet squat usually sets the limit, because holding the bell at your chest gets hard before the swing does.

The table below reflects common coaching practice rather than research, but it matches the loads used in kettlebell studies: 16 kg for men and 8 kg for women in one study ([Hulsey et al. 2012](#references)), and 12 kg for men under 70 kg and 16 kg above in another ([Lake & Lauder 2012](#references)).

| Starting point | Women | Men |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Never lifted, or out of shape | 8 kg | 12 kg |
| Active, some strength training | 12 kg | 16 kg |
| Strong, regular lifter | 16 kg | 20–24 kg |

**Check it before you commit** (borrow bells at a gym or store if you can):

1. **Swing test.** Do 10 two-hand swings. The bell should float up to about chest height from the snap of your hips, with your back flat and your arms relaxed, and you should feel you could do 10 more. If you have to lift it with your arms, or your back rounds, it is too heavy. If it flies above your head with little effort, it is too light.
2. **Goblet squat test.** Hold the bell by the horns at your chest and do 8 slow squats to a comfortable depth, heels down and chest up. You should have 2–3 good reps left at the end. If not, go one size down.

If you are between sizes, take the lighter one for the first month. The swing is a skill, and it is far easier to learn with a bell that does not fight you. If you later buy a second bell, make it 4 kg heavier and use it for swings while the lighter one stays for goblet squats.

## How long to rest

Rest about 2 minutes between ladders at first, and bring it down to 60–90 seconds over the following weeks. Do not go below 60 seconds.

**Within a ladder,** move straight from one exercise to the next. Put the bell down safely, take a breath or two, and go. Alternating already gives each muscle group a little rest: swings rest your chest and arms, push-ups rest your hips.

**Between ladders,** the research on muscle growth sets the floor. A 2024 meta-analysis of randomized trials found a small benefit for resting longer than 60 seconds between sets, and no detectable extra benefit beyond about 90 seconds ([Singer et al. 2024](#references)). The best-known single trial found more muscle growth with 3 minutes than with 1 minute in trained men ([Schoenfeld et al. 2016](#references)), but most people in the meta-analysis were untrained, and in untrained people short and long rests both work. Most of the effect seems to come from shorter rests forcing you to do less work in the next set.

For the endurance side, shorter rest is the main way to make this program harder over time without adding weight. So rest decreases in steps (see the next section), and 60 seconds is where you stop cutting and start adding reps or weight instead.

**Between pairings,** take 2–3 minutes and set up for the next block.

A simple way to run it is on the clock. Start a ladder every 3 minutes in the first weeks. If a ladder takes about 75 seconds, that leaves about 1 minute 45 seconds of rest. Later, start one every 2:30, then every 2:15. Including the warm-up, a session takes about 40 minutes at first and about 30 minutes once rests are short.

A quick self-check: start the next ladder when your breathing has settled enough that you could say a full sentence. If you are still gasping when the clock says go, take the extra 30 seconds.

## The three exercises, scaled for beginners

Choose the version of each exercise you can do for about 8–15 good reps when fresh. The 4-rep rungs then feel easy at the start of the workout and get demanding by the last ladders, which is what you want.

**Swing.** Use the two-hand "hardstyle" swing: hike the bell back between your thighs, snap your hips forward, let the bell float to chest height, and let it fall back into the hike. It is a hip hinge, not a squat and not an arm raise. Spend your first week practising the hinge with kettlebell deadlifts and hike passes before swinging for reps, and if you can, have a coach or an experienced friend check your form.

**Goblet squat.** Hold the bell by the horns at your chest, sit down between your heels, and stand up. Squat only as deep as you can with your back neutral and heels down. When it gets easy, add a 2-second pause at the bottom or a slow 3-second lowering phase before moving to a heavier bell.

**Leg raise.** Lie on your back, hands under your hips, and keep your lower back pressed to the floor throughout.

| Level | Version |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Bent-knee leg raise (or reverse crunch) |
| 2 | Straight-leg raise, lowering slowly |
| 3 | Hanging knee raise from a bar |
| 4 | Hanging straight-leg raise |

If your lower back lifts off the floor, drop a level.

**Push-up.** Body in one straight line, elbows at about 45 degrees to your torso, chest to just above the floor.

| Level | Version |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Hands on a wall or kitchen counter |
| 2 | Hands on a bench or chair seat (lower = harder) |
| 3 | Full push-up from the floor |
| 4 | Feet elevated, or a slow 3-second lowering |

A push-up made hard enough builds muscle about as well as a light bench press. In an 8-week trial, 18 young men did either bench press at 40% of their maximum or push-ups adjusted (by kneeling or raising the hands) to the same load, 3 sets to failure twice a week. Chest and triceps thickness grew by similar amounts in both groups ([Kikuchi & Nakazato 2017](#references)). A shorter 4-week trial in moderately trained men found push-ups increased bench press strength as well as benching did, though neither group's muscle size changed measurably in that time ([Kotarsky et al. 2018](#references)).

## Progressing over 12 weeks

Change one thing at a time: first the ladder length, then the rest, then the exercise variation or the bell.

| Weeks | Ladder | Rest between ladders | Swings per workout |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1–2 | 3-2-1 (6 + 6 reps) | 2 min | 60 |
| 3–5 | 4-3-2-1 (10 + 10 reps) | 2 min | 100 |
| 6–8 | 4-3-2-1 | 90 s | 100 |
| 9–11 | 4-3-2-1 | 60 s | 100 |
| 12 | 3-2-1, easy | 2 min | 60 |

Weeks 1–2 keep the full structure of ten ladders but with shorter ladders, so your back, hands and hips get used to the swing volume gradually. Week 12 is a lighter week to recover before the next block.

**Move to the next step** only when every ladder in the week was done with crisp swings and clean reps, and the last ladder of each workout felt like 7 out of 10 effort or less. If you are not there yet, repeat the week. That is normal.

**After week 12,** you have three options:

- **Heavier bell.** Go up 4 kg and restart at 4-3-2-1 with 2 minutes of rest. This is the best route for building muscle, especially in the legs.
- **Longer ladders.** Keep the bell and move to 5-4-3-2-1 (15 + 15 reps, 150 swings per workout) with 2 minutes of rest. This is the better route for endurance.
- **Harder variations.** Move each bodyweight exercise up a level and drop back to 2 minutes of rest.

**Stop a ladder early** if your swing loses height, your back rounds, or your grip starts to slip. End that ladder, rest longer, and continue. Missing a few reps is far better than practising bad swings.

## What the research says this can do

Kettlebell training reliably builds strength and work capacity in beginners, but the evidence for endurance is mixed and the evidence for muscle growth is thin. This program is a good start, not a complete solution.

### Strength and power

Swings make beginners stronger. In one study, college-age men did 12 rounds of 30 seconds of swings with 30 seconds of rest, twice a week for 6 weeks. Their half-squat maximum rose by about 10% and their vertical jump by about 20%, matching a group that did jump squats ([Lake & Lauder 2012](#references)). In another 6-week study, swings, accelerated swings and goblet squats with a 16 kg bell increased squat and power clean strength, but less than a barbell weightlifting program did (squat about 4% versus 14%), and neither program changed body composition ([Otto et al. 2012](#references)).

In older adults, a 12-week hardstyle kettlebell program (swings, cleans, presses, goblet squats and get-ups) improved grip strength, walking distance in a 6-minute test, deadlift strength and leg lean mass in 32 inactive people aged 59–79. Attendance was over 90%, and there were four minor adverse events ([Meigh et al. 2022](#references)).

### Endurance

Swings drive your heart rate high, but your oxygen use, which is what builds aerobic fitness, lags behind. In 12 minutes of continuous swings with a 16 kg bell, ten men averaged about 87% of maximal heart rate but only about 65% of their maximal oxygen uptake ([Farrar et al. 2010](#references)). When swings and treadmill running were matched for how hard they felt, heart rate was the same, but running used 25–39% more oxygen and energy ([Hulsey et al. 2012](#references)).

Training studies are split:

- In 40 office workers doing kettlebell training three times a week for 8 weeks, neck, shoulder and low-back pain fell and back strength rose, but aerobic fitness did not change ([Jay et al. 2011](#references)).
- In female college soccer players, 20 minutes of 15-second kettlebell snatch intervals three times a week raised VO2max by about 6% in 4 weeks, while a circuit-training group did not improve ([Falatic et al. 2015](#references)). Snatches are more demanding than swings, and the bell went overhead every rep.

A ladder program with rest between ladders behaves more like interval strength training than continuous cardio. If aerobic fitness matters to you, add one or two sessions of brisk walking, cycling or easy running on your off days.

### Muscle growth

This program can build muscle, but only if the sets are hard enough. Muscle grows about as well with light loads as with heavy ones when sets are taken close to failure ([Schoenfeld et al. 2017](#references)). You don't have to reach failure: a meta-analysis found no significant difference between training to failure and stopping a little short ([Refalo et al. 2023](#references)), but growth does improve as sets end closer to failure ([Robinson et al. 2024](#references)).

That is the main weakness of short ladders with a light bell: a 4-rep rung of goblet squats is far from failure. The fatigue that builds up over five ladders helps, but only if the exercise is hard enough to begin with. That is why the exercise variations are chosen at 8–15 fresh reps, and why the progression keeps making things harder.

Combining cardio and strength work in the same session does not seem to hold back muscle growth. A meta-analysis of 43 studies found concurrent training did not reduce gains in muscle size or maximal strength compared with strength training alone, though gains in explosive strength were blunted, especially when both were done in the same session ([Schumann et al. 2022](#references)).

The 2026 position stand from the American College of Sports Medicine puts the big picture plainly: the largest gains come from going from no resistance training to any, training every major muscle group at least twice a week is the target, and bodyweight and home training work ([ACSM 2026](#references)). This program does that for most of the body, with one gap.

## Two gaps to know about

**There is no pulling exercise.** Swings work your grip, back and hips, but nothing in the program trains your lats and biceps through a full pull. For balanced upper-body development, add 2–3 sets of one-arm kettlebell rows (8–12 reps per side) after Day A and Day C, or swap one of each week's push-up ladders for rows.

**The legs will outgrow the bell.** Goblet squats with a beginner bell stop being challenging within a few months for most people. Pauses and slow lowering buy time, but eventually you need a heavier bell, split squats, or a barbell.

## A sample week

Each session: 5–8 minutes of warm-up (hip hinges, bodyweight squats, wall or incline push-ups, 10 light swings), then the blocks below, then optional rows.

| | Day A | Day B | Day C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Block 1 | 5 ladders squat + swing | 5 ladders leg raise + swing | 5 ladders push-up + swing |
| Block 2 | 3 ladders leg raise + swing | 3 ladders push-up + swing | 3 ladders squat + swing |
| Block 3 | 2 ladders push-up + swing | 2 ladders squat + swing | 2 ladders leg raise + swing |
| Optional | Rows 2–3 × 8–12 per side | — | Rows 2–3 × 8–12 per side |

## Bottom line

- **Bell:** women about 8 kg (new) or 12 kg (some training); men about 12 kg (new) or 16 kg (some training). Pick the bell you can goblet squat for 8 controlled reps with 2–3 to spare.
- **Rest:** about 2 minutes between ladders to start, down to 90 and then 60 seconds over the weeks; never below 60 seconds. Take 2–3 minutes between pairings.
- **Start easy:** 3-2-1 ladders for the first two weeks while you learn the swing.
- **Progress** by shortening rest, then by harder variations, a heavier bell or longer ladders.
- **Expect** clear gains in strength and work capacity, modest muscle growth if sets are hard enough, and better aerobic fitness if you add some easy cardio on off days.
