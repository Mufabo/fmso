Zinc is one of the best-studied supplements, and the research points to a simple rule: it helps people who are low in zinc and people with a fresh cold, and does little for anyone else. This post covers which forms to buy, how much to take, what the trials actually show, and where the risks start.

*This post is general information, not medical advice. If you have a health condition or take medications, talk to your doctor or pharmacist before supplementing.*

## What zinc does and how much you need

Adults need 8–11 mg of zinc a day, and most should stay at or below 40 mg a day from all sources. Zinc is needed by hundreds of enzymes and supports immune function, protein and DNA synthesis, wound healing and cell division.[^1] The body holds only about 1.5–2.5 g in total and has no dedicated store, so it needs a steady supply from food.

| Group | Daily need (RDA) | Upper limit (UL) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Men 19+ | 11 mg | 40 mg |
| Women 19+ | 8 mg | 40 mg |
| Pregnancy (19+) | 11 mg | 40 mg |
| Breastfeeding (19+) | 12 mg | 40 mg |
| Teens 14–18 | 9–13 mg | 34 mg |

Source: U.S. Food and Nutrition Board, via the NIH ODS fact sheet.[^1] The upper limit was set from the dose that begins to disturb copper status. It does not apply to people taking zinc under medical supervision.

The best food sources are oysters (about 28–32 mg per 3 oz), beef (about 3.8 mg per 3 oz), crab, pork, cheese and fortified cereals. Beans, nuts and whole grains contain zinc too, but their phytates bind it and cut absorption. Depending on how plant-heavy a diet is, the body absorbs anywhere from about 5% to over 50% of dietary zinc.

## Which form of zinc to buy

Citrate, gluconate and bisglycinate are all good choices, and oxide is the weakest. The dose that matters is **elemental zinc**. U.S. Supplement Facts labels already state elemental zinc rather than the weight of the whole compound.[^1]

| Form | Elemental zinc (approx.) | What the evidence says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zinc citrate | ~31% | About 61% absorbed in healthy adults, the same as gluconate[^2] |
| Zinc gluconate | ~14% | About 61% absorbed; widely used in cold lozenges |
| Zinc acetate | ~30% | Releases free zinc ions easily; the strongest cold-lozenge evidence[^3] |
| Zinc bisglycinate | ~20–27% | A chelate often chosen for gentler digestion; few head-to-head human absorption trials |
| Zinc picolinate | ~20% | One small 1987 study suggested better absorption;[^4] not replicated |
| Zinc sulfate | ~23% | Standard medical form; WHO lists it for childhood diarrhea; more stomach upset |
| Zinc oxide | ~80% | About 50% absorbed; some people absorb almost none[^2] |
| Zinc carnosine (polaprezinc) | ~23% | Used for its local effect on the stomach and gut lining rather than for absorption; an approved ulcer drug in Japan (see the [gut section](#zinc-carnosine-and-gut-permeability) below) |

Elemental percentages are calculated from each compound's molecular weight and vary with the hydrate form used.

The key absorption study is a randomized crossover trial of 15 young adults given 10 mg zinc without food. Median absorption was 61.3% from citrate, 60.9% from gluconate and 49.9% from oxide, and three participants absorbed little or nothing from oxide.[^2] The authors suggest insoluble oxide may dissolve poorly in people with low stomach acid.

Two practical points. Taking 25 mg or more of supplemental iron at the same time reduces zinc absorption.[^1] And as zinc intake rises, the fraction absorbed falls, so megadoses deliver less than their label suggests.

## What zinc actually does: the evidence by use

The best evidence is for treating colds early, slowing macular degeneration and treating diarrhea in children who are low in zinc. Benefits for acne, blood sugar and testosterone are smaller or limited to people who start out deficient.

| Use | Dose studied (elemental zinc) | Result | Strength of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Treating a cold | >75 mg/day as lozenges, started within 24 h | Colds about 2 days or ~33% shorter | Moderate, but debated |
| Preventing colds | Various daily doses | Little or no effect | Low |
| Intermediate macular degeneration (AMD) | 80 mg/day (oxide) + 2 mg copper, with antioxidants | ~25% lower risk of advanced AMD over 5 years | Strong (large RCTs) |
| Diarrhea in children, low-income settings | 10–20 mg/day for 10–14 days | About half a day shorter; fewer cases persisting past 7 days | Moderate (Cochrane) |
| Acne | Often ~30 mg/day | Fewer inflammatory papules | Low to moderate |
| Type 2 diabetes | Mostly 20–50 mg/day | Modest drops in fasting glucose and HbA1c | Low to moderate |
| Testosterone and male fertility | ~30–45 mg/day | Testosterone rises only in deficient men; no gain in sperm quality or live births in the largest trial | Strong for no fertility benefit (large RCT); low for testosterone |

### Colds: the strongest everyday use, with a live debate

Zinc lozenges appear to shorten colds when taken early and at a high enough dose. Hemilä's meta-analysis of seven randomized trials found that acetate and gluconate lozenges giving more than 75 mg/day shortened colds by about 33%. Acetate trials averaged 40% and gluconate 28%, a difference that was not statistically significant.[^3] Lower doses under 75 mg/day showed no effect.[^5]

In three high-dose acetate trials (80–92 mg/day), lozenges shortened nasal discharge, congestion, cough and muscle ache, not just throat symptoms.[^6] The authors suggested lozenges be started within 24 hours of symptoms and used for less than two weeks.

The 2024 Cochrane review was more cautious. Across 34 trials and 8,526 people, zinc may shorten colds by about 2 days, but made little or no difference to preventing them. The authors rated the evidence low to very low certainty and noted more mild side effects such as bad taste and nausea.[^7] Hemilä and Chalker argued that Cochrane pooled very different products and used a poor scale for cold duration; reanalyzing its lozenge trials, they found adult colds shortened by 37%.[^8]

Practical takeaways from the trials: dissolve lozenges slowly in the mouth rather than swallowing them, and avoid products with citric acid or citrate, which bind zinc and stop it being released.

### Macular degeneration (AREDS)

The Age-Related Eye Disease Study gave 80 mg zinc as zinc oxide plus 2 mg copper daily, with or without antioxidant vitamins. In people at high risk of advanced AMD, the full formula cut the risk of advanced disease by about 25% over 5 years.[^9] Copper was included specifically to prevent zinc-induced copper deficiency. AREDS2 hinted that 25 mg zinc may work as well, but the National Eye Institute still recommends the 80 mg formula.[^10] This only applies to intermediate or one-eye advanced AMD and is not a prevention strategy for healthy eyes.

### Childhood diarrhea

A 2016 Cochrane review of 33 trials in 10,841 children found zinc shortened diarrhea by about half a day in children over 6 months, and by about a day in children with signs of malnutrition.[^11] WHO and UNICEF recommend 20 mg/day (10 mg under 6 months) for 10–14 days. The effect is likely marginal in well-nourished children.

### Acne

A 2020 meta-analysis found people with acne had slightly lower serum zinc than controls, and zinc treatment reduced inflammatory papules compared with placebo or other comparators.[^12] Trials were small and varied in dose and form, and oral zinc is generally weaker than standard acne treatments.

### Blood sugar and type 2 diabetes

A meta-analysis of 32 trials found zinc (mean 35 mg/day) lowered fasting glucose, insulin resistance and HbA1c in adults with type 2 diabetes.[^13] The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements still judges the evidence insufficient to recommend zinc for preventing or managing diabetes, because trials are small and inconsistent.

### Testosterone and fertility

Zinc matters for testosterone only when you are short of it. In a small study, restricting zinc in young men cut testosterone sharply over 20 weeks, and supplementing marginally deficient older men roughly doubled their levels.[^14] In zinc-sufficient men, a high-dose zinc/magnesium product (ZMA) did not change testosterone.[^15]

For fertility, the picture is similar: a link with low zinc, but no proven benefit from supplements. Infertile men have lower zinc in their seminal fluid than fertile men on average, and pooled small trials suggested supplements improved sperm motility, shape and semen volume.[^16]

The largest and best-designed trial did not confirm this. In the Folic Acid and Zinc Supplementation Trial (FAZST), 2,370 couples seeking infertility treatment were randomized so the man took 30 mg zinc plus 5 mg folic acid, or placebo, daily for 6 months. Live birth rates were 34% versus 35%, and sperm count, motility and shape did not differ. The supplement group had more stomach upset and slightly more sperm DNA fragmentation.[^17] The authors concluded the combination should not be recommended for male infertility. Zinc may still help a man who is genuinely deficient, which a doctor can check.

For women, routine zinc in pregnancy does not reduce low birthweight or stillbirth but may slightly lower the risk of preterm birth.[^18] Prenatal vitamins usually already contain enough.

## Zinc carnosine and gut permeability

Zinc carnosine (polaprezinc) is the one zinc form with human evidence for protecting the gut lining, but that evidence comes from two very small studies. It bonds zinc to carnosine, a small dipeptide, and has been an approved anti-ulcer drug in Japan since 1994.[^19] Proposed mechanisms include raising heat shock proteins and antioxidant effects in the mucosa.

| Study | Who | Regimen | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mahmood et al., *Gut* 2007[^20] | 10 healthy volunteers, crossover | 37.5 mg twice daily or placebo for 7 days, with the painkiller indomethacin for the last 5 | Indomethacin tripled gut permeability on placebo; no significant rise with zinc carnosine |
| Davison et al., *Am J Clin Nutr* 2016[^21] | 8 volunteers, double-blind crossover | 37.5 mg twice daily, colostrum, both, or placebo for 14 days, then hard exercise | Exercise raised body temperature 2°C and tripled permeability; zinc carnosine or colostrum alone cut the rise by 70% at day 14; the combination added further benefit and already cut it by 30% at day 2 |

The 2016 trial is the only one to test zinc carnosine with bovine colostrum (20 g/day), a natural source of growth factors. In parallel cell experiments, a 2°C temperature rise doubled cell death and weakened the cell barrier three- to fourfold. Each supplement alone reduced that damage by 35–50%, and the pair together worked best.[^22] The authors point to higher heat shock protein 70 and stronger tight junctions between gut cells. The combination has not been tested by an independent group.

The standard dose of 37.5 mg twice daily supplies roughly 16–17 mg of elemental zinc a day, which counts toward the 40 mg upper limit.

The caveats are large. Both trials enrolled ten people or fewer and came from the same research group. They measured a urine marker of permeability over days to weeks, not symptoms or long-term health. And they tested healthy people under a specific short-term stressor, so they say nothing about vague "leaky gut" complaints, IBS or food sensitivities. Zinc carnosine is a reasonable short-term experiment for endurance athletes with gut symptoms. Anyone on long-term NSAIDs should ask their doctor about established stomach-protecting drugs first.

## Who is most likely to benefit

Most people who eat meat, seafood or dairy get enough zinc, but about 15% of U.S. adults fall below the average requirement.[^1] The groups most at risk are:

- **Vegetarians and vegans**, because phytates in legumes and whole grains block absorption. Soaking, sprouting and fermenting help.[^23]
- **People with gut disorders**: 15–40% of people with inflammatory bowel disease are zinc-deficient, as are about half of people newly diagnosed with celiac disease. Bariatric surgery also raises the risk.
- **People with alcohol use disorder**, of whom 30–50% have low zinc status.
- **Pregnant and breastfeeding women**, whose needs rise by 3–4 mg/day.
- **Older adults**, who often have marginal intakes.
- **Children with sickle cell disease** and exclusively breastfed infants over 6 months.

A blood test can help, but serum zinc varies with time of day, inflammation and recent meals, so a single low result is not conclusive.

## Side effects and risks

Short courses of zinc are safe for most people; the real danger is long-term high doses that drain copper.

- **Stomach upset.** Nausea, cramps, vomiting and a metallic taste are the common side effects, usually within 3–10 hours of a large dose. Taking zinc with food helps.
- **Copper deficiency.** Doses of 50 mg/day or more taken for weeks can block copper absorption, lower HDL cholesterol and even weaken immunity.[^1] Severe copper deficiency causes anemia, low white cell counts and nerve damage such as myelopathy, which may not fully recover. Case reports link it to heavy, years-long use of zinc-containing denture creams.[^24] If you take high doses long term, a combined zinc-copper product is sensible.
- **Nasal zinc.** In 2009 the FDA told consumers to stop using three zinc nasal cold products after more than 130 reports of lost sense of smell, which may be permanent. Doctors had described the problem in patients years earlier.[^25] The warning did not cover zinc taken by mouth.

### Drug interactions

| Medication | Interaction | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quinolone antibiotics (e.g. ciprofloxacin) | Zinc and the drug bind each other, reducing absorption of both | Take the antibiotic 2+ hours before or 4–6 hours after zinc |
| Tetracycline antibiotics | Same as above | Same as above |
| Penicillamine (Wilson disease, rheumatoid arthritis) | Zinc reduces the drug's absorption | Separate by at least 1 hour |
| Thiazide diuretics (e.g. hydrochlorothiazide) | Increase zinc loss in urine | Ask your doctor whether to monitor zinc |
| Iron supplements ≥25 mg | Compete with zinc for absorption | Take at different times of day |

Source: NIH ODS.[^1]

## Daily or weekly?

Weekly zinc works in children who are short of it, but it has not been properly tested in healthy adults, so daily dosing remains the safer default.

| Study | Who | Regimen | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brooks et al., *Lancet* 2005[^26] | 1,621 infants, urban Bangladesh | 70 mg once weekly vs placebo, 12 months | Fewer pneumonia cases (199 vs 286); 2 deaths vs 14; copper and hemoglobin unaffected |
| Gupta et al., 2003[^27] | 280 children, rural India | 10 mg on 5 days a week vs 50 mg weekly vs placebo, 16 weeks | Diarrhea halved in both zinc groups; daily and weekly equal |
| Thu et al., *Am J Clin Nutr* 1999[^28] | 163 children, Vietnam | Multinutrient with 5 mg zinc on 5 days a week vs 17 mg weekly | Blood zinc rose equally with daily and weekly |

Three reasons to be cautious about applying this to adults:

- **Absorption falls as the dose rises.** One big weekly dose is absorbed less efficiently than the same total split across seven days.
- **A matching weekly dose is large.** Replacing 10–15 mg/day means 70–100 mg at once, above the 40 mg daily upper limit and more likely to cause nausea. The limit is meant for regular daily intake, so a single weekly dose is not known to be harmful, just untested.
- **Adherence.** Even in the Bangladesh trial, the authors warned a weekly schedule might be hard to keep outside a study.

Weekly dosing does have one possible advantage: a much lower total intake, which may reduce the copper-depletion risk seen with long-term daily high doses. It does not suit cold treatment, which depends on frequent lozenges through the day, or the AREDS formula, which was tested as a daily regimen.

## Bottom line

If you eat a varied diet, you probably don't need a zinc supplement. If you do take one, here is what the evidence supports:

| Goal | Form | Dose (elemental zinc) | Duration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Covering a low intake | Citrate, gluconate or bisglycinate | 10–25 mg/day with food | Ongoing; stay at or under 40 mg/day from all sources |
| Treating a cold | Acetate or gluconate lozenges, no citric acid | Over 75 mg/day, split into lozenges every 2–3 waking hours | Start within 24 hours; stop within 2 weeks |
| Intermediate AMD | AREDS formula (oxide + copper) | 80 mg/day + 2 mg copper | Long term, with an eye doctor's advice |
| Long-term doses above 40 mg | Any, with copper | Add 1–2 mg copper | Only under medical supervision |

Skip zinc nasal sprays, separate zinc from antibiotics, and check with a doctor or pharmacist if you have a medical condition or take regular medication.
