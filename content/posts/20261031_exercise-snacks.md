Three fast climbs up a 60-step stairwell, spread across the day, three days a week. After six weeks, sedentary young adults who did this were measurably fitter than those who didn't. Each climb took about 20 seconds.

That is the idea behind exercise snacks: instead of one workout, you do a few very short bursts of hard effort, separated by hours, on several days a week. The approach has gone from a niche lab question to a field with several meta-analyses in the last two years, plus some striking data from people wearing activity trackers.

This post walks through the strongest studies: what they did, what changed, what didn't, and how much weight the results can bear.

## What counts as an exercise snack?

There is no single agreed definition, and the label covers a few related ideas.

- **Exercise snacks.** Planned, structured bouts of vigorous effort, done at least twice a day and separated by an hour or more. Martin Gibala's group, which did much of the early work, defines them as isolated bouts of vigorous exercise lasting a minute or less.[^1] Other researchers allow up to about 5 minutes; one recent meta-analysis required at least two bouts a day, at least three days a week, for at least two weeks.[^2] Stair climbing, all-out cycling sprints and bodyweight moves are the common formats.
- **VILPA (vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity).** The unplanned version: short bursts of hard effort that happen as part of daily life, like hurrying for a bus, carrying heavy shopping up stairs or a fast walk on an errand. It is measured with wrist-worn activity trackers rather than prescribed.
- **Snacktivity.** A behaviour-change approach that encourages accumulating 2–5 minute bouts of activity, often at moderate rather than vigorous intensity.

The common thread is that the bursts are spread through the day rather than grouped into one session. That separates exercise snacks from classic interval training, where you do several hard bursts with a few minutes of rest in a single workout.

## Where exercise snacks came from: Martin Gibala's research programme

Much of the experimental work on exercise snacks traces back to Martin Gibala, a kinesiology professor at McMaster University in Canada, and his long-time collaborator Jonathan Little at the University of British Columbia Okanagan. Their work follows a clear line: from shrinking interval workouts in the lab, to splitting them across the day, to testing whether people will do them on their own at home.

### Step 1: how little interval training is enough?

Gibala spent more than a decade testing ever-shorter interval protocols. The best-known result is the "one-minute workout" study.

**One minute vs 50 minutes (Gillen et al., 2016).**[^3] Twenty-seven sedentary men were assigned to three weekly sessions of sprint interval training, moderate continuous cycling, or no training for 12 weeks. The sprint group did three 20-second all-out cycle sprints with 2 minutes of easy pedalling between them: 1 minute of hard effort in a 10-minute session including warm-up and cool-down. The moderate group cycled for 45 minutes at about 70% of maximum heart rate (50 minutes in total). VO2peak rose by 19% in both groups, and insulin sensitivity and muscle mitochondrial content improved similarly, despite a five-fold difference in exercise time. Groups were small (9 and 10 people, with 6 controls).

**Taking it to the stairs (Allison et al., 2017).**[^4] The group then checked whether stairs could replace the lab bike. In sedentary young women, three 20-second all-out stair climbs produced heart rate, blood lactate and effort ratings similar to cycling sprints. Twelve women who did the 3 × 20-second stair protocol (with 2 minutes of recovery) three times a week for six weeks raised VO2peak by 12%. A less extreme version, 3 × 60 seconds up and down one flight, raised it by 7%.

### Step 2: spread the sprints across the day

The next question was whether the bursts needed to be close together at all. Two 2019 trials, already described in detail below, answered it: three 20-second stair climbs[^5] or cycling sprints[^6] separated by 1–4 hours improved fitness. The cycling study found the spread-out version worked about as well as doing the same sprints in one session.

In a 2022 review, Islam, Gibala and Little formalised the idea, proposing that snacks of a minute or less could improve fitness and offset some of the harm of long sitting, without equipment or dedicated workout time.[^1] Gibala also co-authored the first UK Biobank VILPA study, which looked at the same kind of brief bursts as they occur naturally in daily life.[^7]

### Step 3: the real world is harder

The lab trials were supervised, and participants often had a stairwell next to the lab. The group's most recent trials asked what happens when people do snacks on their own, prompted by an app. The results are more sobering, and they are some of the most useful in the field.

**Inactive middle-aged adults (Babir et al., 2025).**[^8] Seventy-seven inactive adults (average age 54, mostly women) were randomised for 12 weeks to bodyweight exercise snacks or a placebo group doing stretching snacks of the same length. Both groups were asked to do at least three bouts of a minute or less a day, on at least three days a week, guided by an online platform. Retention was 95% and the snacks proved feasible. But VO2peak did not improve more in the snack group than in the stretching group (difference 0.4 mL/kg/min, 95% CI −0.6 to 1.4), and blood markers of cardiometabolic health did not change either. Participants rated their effort at about 4 out of 10 or lower, well below the hard efforts used in the lab studies.

**Adults with type 2 diabetes (Babir et al., 2026, *Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism*).**[^9] Sixty-nine insufficiently active adults with well-controlled type 2 diabetes (average age 58, HbA1c 6.6%) were randomised for 12 weeks to four 1-minute bouts a day, at least five days a week, of either vigorous bodyweight exercises (squats, lunges, step-ups) or gentle stretching. Participants were encouraged to do a bout 30–60 minutes after meals. Adherence was high in both groups, about 18 bouts a week against a target of 20, and 91% finished the study. This time effort was higher (about 6 out of 10), though heart rate during home sessions (about 73% of predicted maximum) was lower than during supervised familiarisation in the lab (about 80%). The snack group improved the 30-second sit-to-stand test by about 2 more repetitions than the stretching group. HbA1c, fasting glucose, estimated fitness, grip strength and body weight did not differ between groups.

**Ongoing.** A trial of exercise snacks in adults with obesity (four 1-minute vigorous bouts at least five days a week, for 12 weeks) was recruiting in 2025–2026.[^10]

The pattern across the programme is clear. Exercise snacks work when they are genuinely hard, as in the supervised stair and sprint studies. When people do them alone and at a moderate effort, they stick with the habit but the fitness gains largely disappear. Intensity, not just the act of moving, seems to be what matters.

## The big observational picture: VILPA in the UK Biobank

The headline numbers on exercise snacks come from a series of studies by Emmanuel Stamatakis and colleagues, using wrist accelerometer data from tens of thousands of UK Biobank participants who said they did no leisure-time exercise.

**Mortality (Stamatakis et al., 2022, *Nature Medicine*).**[^7] 25,241 non-exercisers, average age 62, were followed for about 7 years, during which 852 died. Compared with people who recorded no VILPA, those at the median of three short bouts a day (1–2 minutes each) had a 38–40% lower risk of death from any cause and from cancer, and a 48–49% lower risk of cardiovascular death. The median daily total of 4.4 minutes was linked to 26–34% lower risk across the three outcomes. The relationship was close to linear: more VILPA, lower risk. In 62,344 people who did exercise, vigorous activity showed similar associations.

**Cancer (Stamatakis et al., 2023, *JAMA Oncology*).**[^11] 22,398 non-exercisers were followed for about 6.7 years, with 2,356 new cancers. Almost all VILPA (92%) came in bouts of a minute or less. The median of 4.5 minutes a day was associated with a 20% lower risk of any cancer (HR 0.80) and a 31% lower risk of the 13 cancers most linked to inactivity (HR 0.69). Around 3.5 minutes a day captured about half of the maximum benefit.

**Heart attacks, heart failure and stroke (Stamatakis et al., 2025, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*).**[^12] Among 13,018 women and 9,350 men followed for about 8 years, the results split by sex. In women, a median of 3.4 minutes of VILPA a day was associated with a 45% lower risk of major cardiovascular events (HR 0.55) and a 67% lower risk of heart failure (HR 0.33). Even 1.2–1.6 minutes a day was linked to a 30% lower risk of major events. In men, the dose-response was weaker and less clear: the median of 5.6 minutes a day was associated with only a 16% lower risk.

### How much should we trust these numbers?

These are well-conducted cohort studies, but they cannot show cause and effect.

- **Reverse causation.** People who are already ill move less vigorously. The authors excluded early events and adjusted for many health factors, but some of the association likely reflects underlying health rather than the activity itself.
- **Who does VILPA.** Someone who naturally takes the stairs two at a time may differ in many ways from someone who doesn't. Adjustment for lifestyle and socioeconomic factors helps, but cannot capture everything.
- **One week of data.** Activity was measured over a single week and assumed to represent years of habit.
- **A specific population.** UK Biobank participants are mostly white, middle-aged to older and healthier than average.

The effect sizes, 40–50% lower risk from a few minutes a day, are larger than most randomised exercise trials would lead you to expect. Treat them as a strong signal that small doses of vigorous activity matter, not as a precise estimate of what four minutes a day will do for you.

## Randomised trials: does fitness actually improve?

Cardiorespiratory fitness, usually measured as VO2peak or VO2max, is one of the strongest predictors of long-term health. It is also the outcome where exercise snack trials are most consistent.

### Stair climbing

**The McMaster stair study (Jenkins et al., 2019).**[^5] Twenty-four sedentary young adults were randomly assigned to a control group or to three vigorous climbs of a 3-flight, 60-step stairwell per day, separated by 1–4 hours, three days a week for six weeks. Each climb took about 20 seconds, after a brief warm-up. VO2peak rose by about 5% compared with the control group, and peak cycling power improved by about 12%. The authors described the gain in fitness as modest but real.

**Stair snacks vs a 40-minute workout (Yin et al., 2024).**[^13] Forty-two inactive young adults were assigned to exercise snacks, moderate-intensity continuous training or a control group for six weeks, three days a week. The snack group did three 30-second all-out climbs of a 6-flight, 126-step stairwell, at least an hour apart. The continuous group cycled for 40 minutes at 60–70% of maximum heart rate. VO2peak rose by about 7% (2.5 mL/kg/min) in the snack group, while the change in the continuous group (about 3%) was not significant. Neither intervention improved the body's maximal fat-burning rate.

**Older stair studies.** The idea is not new. In the early 2000s, Boreham and colleagues showed that sedentary young women who built up to several 2-minute bouts of brisk stair climbing a day, five days a week for 7–8 weeks, improved their fitness.[^14][^15]

### Cycling sprints

**Sprint snacks vs classic sprint intervals (Little et al., 2019).**[^6] Inactive young adults did three 20-second all-out cycle sprints three times a week for six weeks. One group (n = 12) spread the sprints 1–4 hours apart; the other (n = 16) did them in a single 10-minute session with 3 minutes of rest between sprints. VO2peak rose by about 4% in the snack group and 6% in the classic group, and time-trial performance improved by about 9% and 13%, with no significant difference between groups. There was no non-exercising control group, so this study tells us that spreading the sprints out works about as well, not how much either works beyond doing nothing. Interestingly, enjoyment rose only in the single-session group, possibly because the snack group had to come to the lab three times a day.

### What the meta-analyses say

Two recent meta-analyses pooled these and similar trials.

**Rodríguez et al. (2026), *British Journal of Sports Medicine*.**[^2] Eleven randomised trials with 414 physically inactive participants, 69% of them women. Exercise snacks substantially improved cardiorespiratory fitness in adults (Hedges' g = 1.37; g = 1.02 after removing one outlier study), with moderate certainty. Improvements in individual trials ranged from about 5% to 17%, with total exercise time of only about 4.5 to 67.5 minutes per week. Compliance (91%) and adherence (83%) were high. All the adult fitness trials used stair climbing.

**Meng et al. (2026), *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging*.**[^16] Twenty-one randomised trials with 921 adults, using a broader definition that included some protocols with one bout a day. Exercise snacks improved VO2max (g = 1.04) and peak power output (g = 0.68), both with moderate certainty. Three findings stand out:

- **Age.** VO2max improved in studies where participants averaged under 50, but not in studies averaging over 50. Peak power improved in all age groups.
- **Dose.** More bouts per day, 4–5 days a week and interventions of 7–9 weeks were associated with larger fitness gains. These are comparisons between studies, so they are hints, not proof.
- **Publication bias.** The authors found statistical signs of publication bias for VO2max, meaning small negative studies may be missing and the pooled effect may be inflated.

Both reviews agree on the core point: exercise snacks reliably improve aerobic fitness in inactive adults. The gains are moderate in absolute terms, typically 5–10% over 6 weeks, but come from a tiny time commitment.

## Blood sugar: snacks before meals

The most interesting short-term finding is about timing rather than fitness.

**Exercise snacks before meals (Francois et al., 2014, *Diabetologia*).**[^17] Nine adults with insulin resistance (average BMI 36; two were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes during screening) each tried three conditions in random order:

- 30 minutes of moderate incline walking before dinner;
- six 1-minute bouts of fast incline walking at 90% of maximum heart rate, with 1 minute of slow walking between them, finishing 30 minutes before breakfast, lunch and dinner;
- the same snack format, alternating walking with resistance exercises.

Snacking lowered the blood sugar rise after breakfast and after dinner (compared with the single walk), reduced average 24-hour glucose by 0.7 mmol/L, and the lower levels persisted into the following day. The walking-plus-resistance version worked just as well.

This was a small crossover study measuring a single day of exercise, so it shows an acute effect, not a long-term change in diabetes control.

**Real-world snacks in type 2 diabetes (Babir et al., 2026, *Diabetologia*).**[^18] The Gibala and Little groups repeated the idea outside the lab. Adults with well-controlled type 2 diabetes wore a continuous glucose monitor for two 48-hour periods with controlled diets: one with no exercise, and one with four 1-minute bouts of vigorous bodyweight exercise a day, guided by emailed videos. The snacks produced statistically significant but small reductions in glucose spikes after meals and in glucose variability. The authors pointed to the very low exercise dose and participants' already good glucose control as likely reasons the effect was modest.

**Same walking time, split vs in one go (Pinto et al., 2026, *Obesity*).**[^22] This trial asked whether splitting up activity matters when the total amount is the same. Thirty adults with overweight or obesity (average age 34, average BMI 29.5) were randomised to 45 minutes of brisk walking a day, five days a week for six weeks. One group did it as short bouts spread through the day, breaking up long periods of sitting. The other did one continuous 45-minute walk. Both groups ended up with similar overall activity, sitting time and energy expenditure (measured with accelerometers and doubly labelled water), but only the split group spent less time in long unbroken stretches of sitting. Neither approach improved insulin sensitivity. The split group did better on glucose tolerance in a lab test (a smaller glucose area under the curve after an intravenous glucose load). In daily life, measured with continuous glucose monitors, they spent more time in the healthy glucose range (reported as 82% to 94%; odds ratio 7.2 against the single-walk group, though with a very wide confidence interval of 1.1 to 46.1), and their glucose swung up and down less. A second measure of glucose tolerance (the rate at which glucose is cleared from the blood) pointed the same way but was borderline, with a confidence interval just touching zero.

This study is small, and the walking was brisk rather than vigorous, so it sits closer to the "breaking up sitting" research than to the stair-sprint snacks above. But it is the clearest test so far of the spreading-out question on its own: the same 45 minutes, done in pieces, did more for blood sugar control than one block. It did not, however, improve the underlying insulin sensitivity.

Together with the 12-week trial in type 2 diabetes, which found no change in HbA1c, this suggests snacks reliably nudge blood sugar on the days you do them, and spreading activity through the day may give blood sugar an edge over a single session. But a few minutes a day may not be enough to shift long-term control in people whose diabetes is already well managed. It fits with a wider body of research showing that breaking up long periods of sitting with frequent short activity breaks improves blood sugar after meals. Most trials ran for six weeks or less or did not measure glucose, so whether snacking improves markers like HbA1c over months is still an open question.

## Older adults: strength and balance snacks

Most stair and sprint trials recruited young adults. For older adults, researchers have mostly tested home-based strength and balance snacks.

**The Bath pilot (Perkin et al., 2019).**[^19] Twenty healthy older adults (average age about 70) did two 5-minute snacks a day for 28 days at home, without supervision: five leg exercises such as sit-to-stand and single-leg knee bends, each for one minute with one minute of rest. Compared with controls, the snack group increased leg pressing power by about 6% and thigh muscle size by about 2%, and improved the 60-second sit-to-stand test.

**12 weeks in pre-frail older adults (Liang et al., 2024).**[^20] Ninety older adults with impaired strength and balance were randomised to a waitlist or 12 weeks of two daily snacks: one strength snack (five exercises, one minute each) and one Tai-chi-based balance snack. The programme improved lower-limb strength, balance and mobility on the Short Physical Performance Battery, and participants found it acceptable and easy to fit into their day.

**Feasibility.** Other pilots, including a remotely delivered program in Australia, found that older adults stuck with home-based snacks well.[^21] Adherence across the older-adult studies ranged from about 81% to 98%.[^2]

The pooled evidence here is weaker. The BJSM meta-analysis found a small improvement in muscular endurance in older adults (g = 0.40), rated very low certainty, and no significant effect on maximal strength. Bodyweight snacks may simply not provide enough load to build much strength.

## What exercise snacks probably don't do

Across the meta-analyses, the benefits are consistent for fitness and not for much else.

- **Body fat.** No reliable reduction. One meta-analysis even found a small overall increase in body fat percentage, with low certainty and high heterogeneity.[^16] The one trial that did show fat loss used six bouts a day for 12 weeks in adults with obesity.
- **Cholesterol and triglycerides.** No significant changes in either meta-analysis. Most participants started with healthy levels, leaving little room to improve.
- **Blood pressure.** No significant change in the trials that measured it.
- **Strength.** Little effect on maximal strength, at least with bodyweight exercises.

In short, exercise snacks are a fitness tool, not a fat-loss or cholesterol tool.

## How much weight can this evidence bear?

The direction is consistent, but the trials are small and short.

- **Small samples.** Most training groups had 10 to 30 people.
- **Short durations.** Interventions lasted 4 to 12 weeks. We have no randomised data on what happens over a year or more.
- **Young, healthy participants.** Most fitness trials recruited inactive university students. The evidence for VO2max gains in people over 50 is thin.
- **Lab conditions.** Most positive fitness trials were supervised, sometimes in a stairwell next to the lab. The two 12-week real-world trials from Gibala's and Little's groups found people stuck with snacks at home, but neither found a fitness gain over a stretching placebo.[^8][^9] That is the most important caveat in the field.
- **Observational vs experimental.** The dramatic mortality and cancer numbers come from VILPA cohort studies, not from trials. The trials show fitness improves; the cohorts suggest that matters for long-term health. Neither alone proves the full chain.

## What this means for you

If you are currently inactive, exercise snacks are one of the most time-efficient ways to get fitter, with good evidence behind them. If you already train regularly, they are a useful extra rather than a replacement. The table below is a practical reading of the studies above, not a set of tested prescriptions.

| Goal | Example setup | Based on | Who it suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aerobic fitness | 3 bouts a day of 20–30 s fast stair climbing (3–6 flights), at least 1 h apart, 3–5 days a week | Jenkins 2019[^5]; Yin 2024[^13]; Meng 2026[^16] | Inactive adults with access to stairs |
| Aerobic fitness, no stairs | 3 × 20 s all-out on a bike, 1–4 h apart, 3 days a week | Little 2019[^6] | Anyone with a stationary bike |
| Blood sugar after meals | About 6 × 1 min of brisk uphill walking or bodyweight moves, finishing 30 min before main meals | Francois 2014[^17] | People with insulin resistance (acute evidence only) |
| Strength and balance with age | Twice a day, 5 exercises × 1 min (sit-to-stand, knee bends, calf raises, balance work) | Perkin 2019[^19]; Liang 2024[^20] | Older adults, especially if pre-frail |
| Everyday health | Turn daily tasks into a few 1-minute bursts: take stairs fast, walk briskly, carry the shopping | Stamatakis 2022–2025[^7][^11][^12] | Everyone, especially non-exercisers |

A few practical points from the trials:

- **Intensity is the point.** The stair and sprint studies that improved fitness used vigorous or all-out effort. In the real-world trials, where people chose their own effort, fitness did not improve. Aim for at least 7 out of 10: you should be breathing hard and unable to talk by the end of the bout.
- **Spread them out.** Most protocols left at least an hour between bouts. That is what makes them snacks rather than a short workout.
- **More often seems better.** Between-study comparisons suggest more bouts per day and 4–5 days a week give larger fitness gains.
- **Warm up briefly.** Several protocols included a short warm-up of jumping jacks, squats or lunges before each bout.

If you are older, have a heart condition or have not exercised in a long time, talk to your doctor first, and start at a hard-but-controlled pace rather than all-out.
