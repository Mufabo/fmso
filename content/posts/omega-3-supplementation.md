Omega-3 fish oil is one of the most popular supplements in the world, and one of the most argued about. The short version: eating oily fish has a strong track record, while capsules help some specific groups (people with existing heart disease, possibly depression, possibly older adults trying to hold on to muscle) and do surprisingly little for the hearts of healthy people. This post covers what omega-3s are, what the trials show for the heart, brain and muscle, how to pick a product, fish versus vegan sources, and food versus pills.

*This post is general information, not medical advice. If you have a health condition or take medications, especially blood thinners, talk to your doctor or pharmacist before supplementing.*

## The three omega-3s that matter

There are three main omega-3 fatty acids, and they are not interchangeable[^1]:

| Omega-3 | Carbons | Main sources | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ALA (alpha-linolenic acid) | 18 | Flaxseed, chia, walnuts, canola and soybean oil | The only *essential* omega-3; the body cannot make it |
| EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) | 20 | Oily fish, fish oil, krill oil, some algal oils | Most linked to triglyceride lowering and mood effects |
| DHA (docosahexaenoic acid) | 22 | Oily fish, fish oil, algal oil | Concentrated in the brain, retina and sperm |

The body can turn ALA into EPA and then DHA, but the conversion happens mainly in the liver and is very limited, with reported rates below 15%[^1]. In practice, eating EPA and DHA directly (from food or supplements) is the only realistic way to raise them.

A detail that matters for vegans: fish don't make EPA and DHA themselves. Microalgae at the base of the marine food chain synthesize them, and fish accumulate them by eating up that chain[^1]. Algal oil simply skips the fish.

Official recommendations exist only for ALA (1.6 g/day for men and 1.1 g/day for women). No formal requirement has been set for EPA or DHA[^1].

### Measuring your status: the Omega-3 Index

The Omega-3 Index is the share of EPA plus DHA in your red blood cell membranes. Because red cells live about 120 days, it reflects your intake over roughly the previous four months, unlike plasma levels, which swing with your last meal[^1]. Western populations who eat little fish typically sit around 3–5%; in Japan, where fish intake is high, levels are about twice as high[^1].

Researchers led by William Harris propose 8% or higher as a cardioprotective target. In one analysis, people who ate no fish and took no supplement averaged about 4.1%, while people eating three fish meals a week *and* taking a supplement averaged about 8.1%. A pooled analysis of 10 cohorts found people at 8% or above were 35% less likely to die of cardiovascular disease than those below 4%[^2]. That is observational data, and the 8% target has not been validated in a trial, but it is a useful way to know whether what you're doing is actually moving the needle. Home finger-prick tests are widely available.

## Omega-3, -6 and -9

Omega-3 and omega-6 are both essential fats that compete with each other inside the body. Omega-9 sits apart, because your body can make it.

| | Omega-3 | Omega-6 | Omega-9 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Main fatty acid | ALA, EPA, DHA | Linoleic acid (LA), arachidonic acid | Oleic acid |
| Essential? | Yes (ALA) | Yes (LA) | No, the body makes it |
| Main sources | Oily fish, algae, flax, chia, walnuts | Sunflower, corn, soybean and safflower oils, nuts, seeds, meat, eggs | Olive oil, canola, avocado, almonds |

### How omega-3 and omega-6 interact

The two families use the same enzymes to turn ALA and LA into longer-chain forms, and those longer-chain forms partly push in opposite directions on inflammation, blood vessel tone and clotting[^43]. That competition is why a high LA intake reduces how much ALA becomes EPA (see [Can you boost ALA conversion?](#can-you-boost-ala-conversion)).

### The ratio debate

- **The case for worrying about it.** Artemis Simopoulos argues that humans evolved on a diet with roughly a 1:1 ratio of omega-6 to omega-3, while Western diets now run around 15:1, and links that shift to inflammation and chronic disease[^42].
- **The case against.** William Harris, the researcher behind the Omega-3 Index, reviewed the evidence and concluded the ratio is of little value. One problem is that it can't tell apart two very different diets: one low in omega-6 and one high in omega-3 can give the same ratio. He also found few human trials supporting it, and argued that EPA and DHA levels alone are a better measure[^43].
- **The AHA's view.** A 2009 advisory recommended at least 5–10% of calories from omega-6. It said cutting omega-6 would more likely raise heart disease risk than lower it, especially when omega-6 replaces saturated or trans fat. It also said omega-6 shouldn't be considered pro-inflammatory[^44].
- **The trial data on inflammation.** A 2017 meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials (1,377 people) found that eating more LA didn't change TNF, IL-6 or most other inflammation markers, and had no overall effect on CRP. Subgroup analyses hinted that CRP might rise with very large increases in LA[^45].

For most people, the lever that matters is raising EPA and DHA, not fighting omega-6. The exception is vegans who rely on ALA conversion: for them, swapping sunflower or corn oil for olive or canola oil improves EPA production.

### Omega-9

Omega-9 is mostly oleic acid, the main fat in olive oil. Because the body makes it, there's no reason to supplement it.

The best evidence for oleic-rich fats comes from whole diets. In PREDIMED, a trial of 7,447 people at high cardiovascular risk, a Mediterranean diet with extra virgin olive oil or extra nuts cut cardiovascular events by about 30% compared with a low-fat control diet. The result held after the original paper was retracted and reanalysed because of randomization errors[^46]. Within the trial, rising blood oleic acid tracked with lower rates of metabolic syndrome, though that part is observational[^47]. The benefit belongs to the whole diet and the olive oil's polyphenols, not to oleic acid alone.

## Heart: food yes, capsules mostly no (with exceptions)

This is where the story is messiest, so here is the summary first:

| Who | What the evidence shows |
| --- | --- |
| Healthy adults, ~1 g/day fish oil | No reduction in major cardiovascular events (VITAL); possibly fewer heart attacks |
| High-risk patients on statins, 4 g/day purified EPA | 25% fewer cardiovascular events (REDUCE-IT), but the placebo is disputed |
| High-risk patients on statins, 4 g/day EPA+DHA mix | No benefit (STRENGTH) |
| Anyone, especially above ~1 g/day | Small, dose-dependent rise in atrial fibrillation |
| People eating 1–2 servings of fish per week | Lower risk of coronary heart disease, sudden cardiac death and ischemic stroke |

### Primary prevention: the VITAL trial

VITAL randomized 25,871 US adults (men 50+, women 55+) to 1 g/day of fish oil or placebo for a median of 5.3 years. Fish oil did not reduce the main composite of heart attack, stroke and cardiovascular death (hazard ratio 0.92, not significant), nor cancer[^3]. One secondary result stood out: total heart attacks were 28% lower (HR 0.72), with the biggest effect in people who ate little fish[^3]. Because it was a secondary endpoint, most cardiologists treat it as a hypothesis, not a finding.

The American Heart Association does not recommend omega-3 supplements for people who aren't at high cardiovascular risk[^1].

### High-risk patients: REDUCE-IT vs STRENGTH

REDUCE-IT gave 8,179 statin-treated patients with raised triglycerides 4 g/day of icosapent ethyl (a prescription, purified EPA ethyl ester sold as Vascepa) or a mineral oil placebo. Cardiovascular events fell by 25%, and the drug was approved in the US as an add-on therapy for people with triglycerides of 150 mg/dL or more[^4].

Then STRENGTH tested 4 g/day of an EPA+DHA mix against a corn oil placebo in a similar high-risk population and found no benefit. A follow-up analysis also found that people who reached higher blood EPA levels did no better[^5].

The fight is over the placebo. In REDUCE-IT, LDL cholesterol, apolipoprotein B and the inflammation marker hsCRP *rose* in the mineral oil group, which critics such as Steven Nissen argue made the placebo group worse rather than the EPA group better[^4]. REDUCE-IT's lead author, Deepak Bhatt, counters that regulators reviewed mineral oil as an acceptable placebo and that the biomarker changes are too small to explain a 25% effect[^4]. It may also be that pure, high-dose EPA works differently from EPA+DHA blends. No trial with a neutral placebo has settled it.

Two practical points regardless of who is right: these are 4 g/day prescription doses in sick people, not a reason for healthy people to take fish oil; and over-the-counter capsules are not Vascepa.

### The atrial fibrillation question

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the one consistent cardiovascular *risk* signal. A 2021 meta-analysis of seven trials in *Circulation* found omega-3 supplements raised AF risk by about 25% overall, with a larger increase at doses above 1 g/day (HR 1.49) than at or below 1 g/day (HR 1.12); each extra gram per day added roughly 11% to the risk[^6].

A UK Biobank study of 415,737 people followed for about 12 years found regular fish oil use in people *without* heart disease was linked to a 13% higher risk of a first AF diagnosis and 5% higher risk of a first stroke. In people who *already had* cardiovascular disease, it was linked to better outcomes: slower progression from AF to heart attack and from heart failure to death[^7]. This was observational, with self-reported use and no dose data, so it can't prove cause.

The most recent and largest analysis is more reassuring for typical supplement doses. A 2026 meta-analysis in *Circulation: Arrhythmia and Electrophysiology* pooled 35 trials and 114,592 people, including previously unpublished AF data, and found doses under 1,500 mg/day of EPA+DHA were not associated with higher AF risk, even in people at high cardiovascular risk. The modest increase appeared only with high-dose (typically prescription) therapy in people with established cardiovascular disease[^8]. Note that this group, the Fatty Acid Research Institute, is closely tied to omega-3 research advocacy.

A reasonable reading: at 1 g/day or less, the AF risk is small to none; at prescription doses, it's real and should be weighed against benefit. If you have AF or palpitations, talk to your doctor before taking fish oil.

## Brain: mood yes (EPA), memory not proven

DHA is a major structural fat in the brain and retina[^1], which makes it tempting to assume more is better for the brain. The trials are more specific than that.

### Depression

The best brain evidence is for depression, and it points to EPA, not DHA.

- A 2011 meta-analysis of 15 trials (916 people) found supplements with at least 60% EPA (of total EPA+DHA) improved depression scores, while those under 60% EPA did nothing. The effective range was roughly 200–2,200 mg/day of EPA in excess of DHA[^9].
- A 2019 meta-analysis of 26 trials (2,160 people) confirmed an overall benefit for EPA-dominant formulas at 1 g/day or more, with no benefit from DHA-dominant ones[^10].
- A 2023 meta-analysis found the same pattern and suggested EPA-rich doses between 1 and 2 g/day; there was too little data to say anything about anxiety[^11].

Most of these trials used omega-3s alongside antidepressants or other treatment. They are an add-on, not a replacement for care.

### Memory and dementia

Observational studies link fish intake to lower dementia risk, but trials of supplements in healthy older adults have not shown that capsules prevent cognitive decline. A Cochrane-based review concluded there was no evidence to support routine omega-3 supplements to prevent or slow cognitive decline in later life[^12]. A 2024 systematic review of 11 trials in people with mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's found some benefits for memory and hippocampal volume in early-stage decline, but other trials found negligible effects[^13]. Trials may be too short to detect a slow process, so the question isn't closed, but "fish oil for memory" is not supported today.

### Pregnancy and the child's brain

A 2026 report from the Danish COPSAC2010 trial is the first randomized test of whether fish oil in pregnancy changes how a child's brain uses energy years later[^36]. In this trial, 700 pregnant women were randomized at week 24 to either 2.4 g/day of long-chain omega-3s (55% EPA, 37% DHA, in triglyceride form) or an olive oil placebo, taken until one week after birth. At age 10, 487 children had brain MRI scans, and 460 had usable data.

| Measure at age 10 | Fish oil vs placebo |
| --- | --- |
| Cerebral blood flow | Lower (−2.3 ml/100 g/min, p = 0.029) |
| Brain oxygen consumption (CMRO₂) | Lower (−17.4 μmol/100 g/min, p = 0.032) |
| Brain lactate | No difference |
| Psychiatric diagnoses, symptom scores, IQ, executive function | Not associated with any of the brain measures |

Lower doesn't mean worse here. Brain blood flow and oxygen use peak in middle childhood and then fall toward adult levels. The researchers confirmed this pattern by comparing the children with 248 adults scanned on the same machine. They interpret the fish oil group's lower values as a shift toward a more mature metabolic profile[^36].

The same trial has reported other effects in earlier papers. Fish oil lengthened pregnancies slightly. Children in the fish oil group had better early language development, and boys reached gross motor milestones earlier. There was no consistent effect on behavioral problems at age 6[^36].

Keep the result in proportion:

- **The effects were small.** The children were scanned only once, and the scans measured whole-brain blood flow and lactate in a single region.
- **No real-world difference showed up.** The brain changes did not translate into any measurable difference in behavior, mental health or cognition at age 10.
- **One trial, high dose.** This is a single Danish trial using a dose well above typical prenatal supplements.

It adds biological plausibility to the idea that omega-3s in late pregnancy shape brain development. It is not yet evidence of a practical benefit for the child. The standard advice still stands: eat 8–12 ounces (about 225–340 g) a week of low-mercury fish during pregnancy[^37], and discuss any supplement with your midwife or doctor.

## Muscle: a modest helper for older adults

The muscle story starts with a small but well-designed trial. Smith and colleagues gave 16 healthy adults aged 65–85 either 4 g/day of fish oil (1.86 g EPA + 1.5 g DHA) or corn oil for 8 weeks. Fish oil did not change muscle protein synthesis at rest, but it roughly tripled the *increase* in protein synthesis when amino acids and insulin were raised, as after a meal, along with stronger activation of the mTOR growth pathway[^14]. Ageing muscle becomes resistant to the anabolic signal of protein, and omega-3s seem to partly restore it.

The same group repeated the experiment in nine healthy adults aged 25–45, using 4 g/day of Lovaza (a prescription fish oil) for 8 weeks. Again, protein synthesis at rest didn't change. During the amino acid and insulin infusion, however, it rose from 0.062 to 0.083 %/h, and mTOR signalling increased by about 50%. Muscle protein concentration and cell size were also greater after supplementation[^38]. So the effect is not limited to older muscle.

Two Canadian studies looked at what fish oil does *inside* muscle cells. Both used 12 weeks of supplementation in healthy young men, with muscle biopsies before and after:

- **Mitochondria.** In 18 men, EPA and DHA in mitochondrial membranes rose by roughly 450% and 320%, displacing some omega-6 fats. Maximal respiration didn't change, but mitochondria became more sensitive to ADP, the signal to make more energy. Their capacity to emit reactive oxygen species rose too, without signs of oxidative damage[^39].
- **Different membranes respond differently.** A follow-up analysis from the same project found EPA and DHA roughly tripled in whole muscle and mitochondrial membranes, cutting the omega-6/omega-3 ratio about threefold. In the cell's outer membrane (the sarcolemma), DHA rose but EPA did not[^40].

These are mechanism studies: they show omega-3s reshape muscle membranes and tweak energy metabolism, not that they make anyone stronger. (The two papers report the dose differently: 2 g EPA + 1 g DHA per day in the mitochondria paper, and 3 g EPA + 2 g DHA per day in the follow-up.)

The clearest functional trial pairs fish oil with training. Forty-five women aged about 64 did 12 weeks of strength training, either alone or with 2 g/day of fish oil, which works out to roughly 0.36 g EPA + 0.24 g DHA. One fish oil group took it during the 90 days of training, and another started 60 days earlier. Both fish oil groups gained more muscle strength and functional capacity than training alone[^41]. The dose is modest, but the trial was small, and the training-only group received no placebo capsule.

Meta-analyses suggest the real-world effect is modest:

| Meta-analysis | Population | Findings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Nutrients*, 2020[^15] | 10 trials, adults 60+ | +0.33 kg muscle mass overall; +0.67 kg at doses above 2 g/day; slightly faster timed-up-and-go; better walking speed with 6+ months of use; no change in grip or leg-press strength |
| Cornish et al., *Nutrients*, 2022[^16] | 16 studies, adults 55+ | No effect on lean mass; improved lower-body strength, timed-up-and-go and sit-to-stand; no effect on walking or upper-body strength |

The consistent thread: if there's a benefit, it shows up at higher doses (around 2–4 g/day), over months, in older adults, and most clearly on functional measures. Resistance training and adequate protein remain the main tools; omega-3s are a possible add-on, not a substitute. There is little evidence that fish oil builds muscle in young, well-fed lifters.

## What to look for in a product

### 1. Read EPA + DHA, not "fish oil"

A "1,000 mg fish oil" capsule often contains only about 300 mg of EPA plus DHA. The number that matters is the EPA and DHA per serving on the Supplement Facts panel. Match the dose and ratio to your goal:

| Goal | What the evidence used |
| --- | --- |
| General intake if you eat little fish | ~250–500 mg/day EPA+DHA |
| Raising a low Omega-3 Index | ~1 g/day EPA+DHA, then retest after 3–4 months |
| Depression (alongside treatment) | 1–2 g/day, at least 60% EPA |
| Muscle in older adults | ~2–4 g/day EPA+DHA, for months |
| Very high triglycerides / high CV risk | Prescription products, under a doctor |

### 2. Chemical form and absorption

| Form | What it is | Absorption |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural triglyceride (TG) | Oil as it occurs in fish | Good; the reference form |
| Ethyl ester (EE) | Concentrated by reacting the oil with ethanol; cheapest way to reach high concentrations | Poor on an empty stomach, much better with a fatty meal |
| Re-esterified triglyceride (rTG) | Ethyl ester converted back to triglyceride | Similar to or better than natural TG |
| Phospholipid (krill oil) | Bound to phospholipids | Good, but low EPA+DHA per capsule and expensive |

Early studies found ethyl esters were absorbed much less than triglycerides, especially without food, while the gap narrowed after a high-fat meal[^17]. A study giving large test doses with meals found both forms equally well absorbed[^18]. Prescription work shows the same pattern: ethyl ester absorption depends heavily on dietary fat[^19]. The practical rule is simpler than the marketing: **take any omega-3 with your largest, fattiest meal**, and an ethyl ester becomes a perfectly reasonable, cheaper choice.

### 3. Freshness (oxidation)

EPA and DHA are highly unsaturated and go rancid easily. The industry body GOED sets voluntary limits: peroxide value ≤5, anisidine value ≤20, and total oxidation (TOTOX) ≤26[^20].

A multi-year analysis of 72 US omega-3 supplements found 68% of *flavored* products exceeded the TOTOX limit, versus 13% of unflavored ones; flavorings can also distort the anisidine test[^20]. A GOED-led survey of unflavored US products found most were within limits[^21]. The health effects of mildly oxidized fish oil aren't well established, but there's no reason to pay for rancid oil.

- Prefer **unflavored** products, or at least ones with published oxidation results.
- Smell or bite a capsule: a strong, sharp fishy or paint-like smell suggests oxidation.
- Look for added antioxidants (vitamin E/tocopherols, rosemary extract).
- Store liquid oils in the fridge after opening; keep capsules cool and dark.

### 4. Purity and third-party testing

Molecular distillation removes most contaminants. A globally representative sample of fish oil supplements found mercury, lead and cadmium at trace levels and dioxins and PCBs within GOED's limits; less concentrated oils carried more contaminants than high-concentrate ones[^22]. In other words, refined fish oil is usually *cleaner* than the fish it came from.

Since labels don't show oxidation or contaminant data, independent certification is the shortcut: look for IFOS, USP Verified, NSF, or similar seals, or a brand that publishes batch-specific certificates of analysis.

### 5. Watch-outs

- **Cod liver oil** is not the same as fish oil. It contains vitamins A and D, and vitamin A can be toxic at high doses, so don't stack it to reach high omega-3 doses[^23].
- **Blood thinners and NSAIDs**: discuss omega-3 supplements with your doctor if you take warfarin, antiplatelet drugs or similar[^23]. In VITAL, 1 g/day did not increase bleeding in the general population[^3].
- **"Omega 3-6-9" blends** dilute the EPA and DHA you want with omega-6 you already get plenty of and omega-9 your body makes. Buy a plain EPA+DHA product instead.
- **Gummies and flavored liquids** tend to have low doses and, as above, higher oxidation.
- **Sustainability**: look for MSC-certified small fish (anchovy, sardine, mackerel) or choose algal oil.

## Fish vs vegan sources

| | Fish / krill oil | Algal oil | ALA (flax, chia, walnut) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Provides | EPA + DHA | DHA, often some EPA (check label) | ALA only |
| Raises blood EPA/DHA | Yes | Yes | Barely (<15% conversion) |
| Contaminant risk | Low if refined | Very low (grown in tanks) | None |
| Sustainability | Depends on fishery | High | High |
| Cost per gram EPA+DHA | Lowest | Higher | N/A |

Algal oil works. A randomized trial found 600 mg/day of DHA from algal-oil capsules raised DHA in blood and red cells just as much as the same amount from cooked salmon[^24], and different algal oils and an algal-fortified food were bioequivalent for DHA[^25].

Two caveats for vegans. First, ALA from flax or chia is good for you in its own right, but it is not a practical way to get EPA and DHA because conversion is so limited[^1]. Second, many algal oils are DHA-dominant. If your goal is mood support, where the evidence favors EPA, look for an algal product that lists a meaningful amount of EPA.

### Can you boost ALA conversion?

A little, and mostly for EPA. Studies that tracked labelled ALA through the body estimate conversion at about 5% to EPA and only about 0.5% to DHA[^28]. These levers move the numbers, roughly in order of usefulness:

1. **Cut omega-6 linoleic acid while keeping ALA up.** Linoleic acid (LA) competes with ALA for the same desaturase and elongase enzymes[^28]. A systematic review found that raising ALA while lowering LA was the most effective dietary strategy: 5 of 7 studies raised EPA, but only 2 of 7 also raised DHA[^29]. In a controlled trial in healthy men, ALA was held at about 1% of energy while LA was varied to give LA:ALA ratios of 4:1 or 10:1, and the low-LA diet produced more EPA[^30]. In practice:
   - Cook with olive oil or canola oil rather than sunflower, corn, safflower or soybean oil.
   - Limit processed foods made with seed oils.
   - Get ALA from flax, chia, hemp seeds and walnuts.
2. **Eat more ALA.** More raw material gives somewhat more EPA. A review of vegetarian diets suggests vegetarians double the standard adequate intake of ALA if they get no direct EPA or DHA[^31].
3. **Use an oil rich in stearidonic acid (SDA).** SDA is the step after ALA, so it skips the slow first desaturation. Ahiflower oil is the richest natural source (about 20% SDA), and echium oil contains about 12–14%. In a 28-day randomized trial, Ahiflower oil raised blood EPA up to four times more than flaxseed oil[^32]. Across SDA studies, tissue EPA rises but DHA does not[^32][^33].
4. **Factors you can't change.** Premenopausal women convert more, likely because estrogen upregulates the conversion enzymes[^34]. Genes matter too: carriers of common minor variants in the *FADS* genes tend to have lower blood EPA and DHA[^28].
5. **Starting low helps.** In a year-long study of omnivores, flexitarians, vegetarians and vegans taking about 3 g/day of ALA from flaxseed oil, the strongest predictor of response was baseline status. People who started with low EPA gained far more EPA (+62.9% vs +12.9%) and DHA (+27.0% vs +7.6%). In that study, sex, age and omega-6 status did not predict conversion[^35].

The catch: none of these strategies reliably raises DHA. The practical vegan plan is plenty of ALA, less omega-6 from seed oils, and an algal oil for DHA (and EPA, if the product contains it)[^31].

## Whole foods vs supplements

For heart health, food has the better evidence. The AHA's 2018 science advisory recommends one to two servings of non-fried seafood per week, linking it to lower risk of coronary heart disease, sudden cardiac death, heart failure and ischemic stroke, especially when fish *replaces* less healthy foods like processed meat[^26]. The advisory notes eating more than that showed no clear extra benefit, and concludes that the benefits of fish outweigh mercury concerns for most adults if you vary the species[^26]. The AHA's separate review of supplements did not recommend them for the general public[^27].

Why might fish beat capsules?

- **It's a package.** Fish also delivers protein, vitamin D, vitamin B12, selenium and iodine.
- **Substitution.** Some of the benefit likely comes from what fish replaces on the plate. The AHA advisory notes the risk reduction is large when fish replaces processed meat and smaller, or absent, when it replaces healthy vegetarian meals[^26].
- **Confounding in the other direction too.** Fish eaters tend to be healthier in other ways, which inflates observational associations. That's exactly why the capsule trials matter, and why their mostly null results are informative.

Practical tips for food:

- **Best choices:** salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring, anchovies, trout. Smaller, oily fish are high in omega-3s and low in mercury.
- **Limit high-mercury species** such as shark, swordfish and king mackerel, especially if pregnant or feeding young children[^26].
- **Not fried.** Fried fish didn't carry the same benefit in the AHA review[^26].
- **Farmed vs wild:** farmed salmon is often *higher* in EPA and DHA than wild, but its content depends on the feed and has fallen as feeds moved away from marine ingredients[^1].

Where supplements make sense: if you don't eat fish, are vegan, have a low Omega-3 Index on testing, have depression (as an add-on, with EPA-rich formulas), are an older adult working on muscle and function, or your doctor prescribes high-dose omega-3s for triglycerides or cardiovascular risk.

## Bottom line

| If you... | Then... |
| --- | --- |
| Eat oily fish 2+ times a week | You probably don't need a supplement. Test your Omega-3 Index if curious. |
| Rarely eat fish | Eat more, or take ~250–1,000 mg/day EPA+DHA with a meal |
| Are vegan or vegetarian | Algal oil (check the EPA content); eat ALA and cut omega-6 seed oils, but don't rely on conversion for DHA |
| Are healthy and want heart protection | Food first; capsules didn't prevent heart attacks or strokes in healthy people |
| Have heart disease or high triglycerides | Ask your doctor about prescription options; don't self-dose at 4 g/day |
| Have depression | Ask about 1–2 g/day of an EPA-dominant (≥60% EPA) formula alongside treatment |
| Are 60+ and want to keep muscle | Strength training and protein first; 2–4 g/day may add a little |
| Are pregnant | 8–12 oz a week of low-mercury fish; ask your midwife or doctor before adding a supplement |
| Have AF or take blood thinners | Talk to your doctor first; stay at or below ~1 g/day unless advised otherwise |

When buying: compare EPA+DHA per serving, choose unflavored, look for third-party testing, take it with your biggest meal, and keep it cool.
