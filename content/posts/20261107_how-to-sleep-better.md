Most sleep advice online is a long list of tips presented as equally important. They aren't. Some are backed by meta-analyses of randomized trials; others rest on a single study of a dozen people. This post sorts the advice by how strong the evidence is, so you know where to spend your effort.

## How much sleep is enough?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society convened a 15-expert panel to review the evidence and concluded that adults should regularly get **7 or more hours** per night. Regularly sleeping less is linked to impaired immune function, more pain, worse performance and a higher risk of accidents. Sleeping more than 9 hours may be fine for young adults, people paying back sleep debt, or people who are ill.[^1]

## 1. Keep a consistent schedule

When you sleep may matter as much as how long. A UK Biobank study tracked more than 60,000 adults with wrist accelerometers and measured how consistent their sleep–wake timing was from day to day. People with the most regular sleep had a substantially lower risk of death over the follow-up period than those with the least regular sleep, and regularity predicted mortality better than sleep duration did.[^2]

This is an observational study, so it can't prove that fixing your schedule will extend your life. Irregular sleep might partly be a marker of poor health. Still, a fixed wake-up time, including weekends, is free, low-risk, and also anchors everything else in this list.

**Try:** Pick a wake time you can keep seven days a week, and get up at that time even after a bad night.

## 2. If you have insomnia, CBT-I is the first-line treatment

This is the best-supported recommendation in the whole field. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) combines several techniques: stimulus control (bed is for sleep, get up if you can't sleep), sleep restriction (temporarily limiting time in bed to consolidate sleep), working on unhelpful thoughts about sleep, sleep hygiene, and relaxation.

A meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials with 1,162 adults found that CBT-I cut the time it took to fall asleep by about 19 minutes and reduced time awake during the night by about 26 minutes, with gains that appeared to last and no reported adverse effects.[^3]

On the strength of this kind of evidence, the American College of Physicians recommends that **all adults with chronic insomnia receive CBT-I as the initial treatment**, ahead of sleeping pills. It's a strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence.[^4]

**Try:** Ask your doctor for a CBT-I referral, or look for a structured digital CBT-I program. It typically takes four to eight weeks.

## 3. Cut caffeine earlier than you think

In a small but well-designed placebo-controlled study, 12 healthy sleepers took 400 mg of caffeine (roughly two to three cups of coffee) at bedtime, 3 hours before, or 6 hours before. Even the dose taken 6 hours before bed significantly disrupted sleep, and objectively measured total sleep dropped by more than an hour. The participants' own sleep diaries mostly didn't register the loss.[^5]

The sample is small and the dose is large, so treat the exact numbers cautiously. The practical takeaway is robust, though: afternoon coffee affects your night more than you notice.

**Try:** No substantial caffeine within 6 hours of bedtime. For an 11 pm bedtime, that means nothing after about 5 pm, and earlier is better.

## 4. Don't use alcohol as a sleep aid

A review of 20 lab studies of healthy volunteers found that alcohol, at every dose, helps you fall asleep faster and makes the first half of the night deeper. The cost comes later: sleep in the second half of the night is more disrupted, the first period of REM sleep is delayed, and moderate to high doses reduce REM across the whole night.[^6]

In short, a nightcap feels like it helps but trades the second half of your night for the first.

**Try:** If you drink, do it earlier in the evening and keep it light, especially on nights when sleep matters.

## 5. Dim the screens before bed

In a controlled inpatient study, adults read for several hours before bed either on a light-emitting tablet or from a printed book. On the tablet they took longer to fall asleep, felt less sleepy in the evening, produced less melatonin, had a later body clock, and were less alert the next morning.[^7]

Again, this was a small study (12 participants) with long reading sessions, so real-world effects may be smaller. But the direction is consistent with what's known about light and the circadian clock.

**Try:** In the last hour before bed, use paper books, dim warm lighting, or at least turn screen brightness right down.

## 6. Exercise regularly

A meta-analysis of 66 studies found that regular exercise has small benefits on total sleep time and sleep efficiency, small-to-moderate benefits on how quickly you fall asleep, and moderate benefits on overall sleep quality. Even a single bout of exercise had small positive effects on that night's sleep.[^8]

**Try:** Regular activity of almost any kind. The effects aren't dramatic, but exercise has so many other benefits that it's an easy win.

### Does it matter when you exercise?

Less than the folk wisdom suggests, with one exception: **hard exercise close to bedtime**.

- **Moderate evening exercise is fine.** A meta-analysis of 23 controlled studies compared nights after a single evening workout with nights without one. Evening exercise slightly *increased* deep sleep and didn't harm sleep overall. The only warning sign was vigorous exercise that ended an hour or less before bed, which may delay falling asleep and reduce sleep time and efficiency.[^17]
- **Even intense exercise is fine if it ends 2–4 hours before bed.** A meta-analysis of 15 studies of high-intensity evening exercise in healthy, good sleepers found no disruption to sleep when it ended 2–4 hours before bedtime. The one consistent change was a small reduction in REM sleep.[^18]
- **Real-world data points to a wider buffer for hard sessions.** A 2025 study analysed more than 4 million nights from 14,689 people wearing fitness trackers. Later and more strenuous workouts were linked to falling asleep later, sleeping less and worse, and a higher heart rate overnight. Workouts ending at least 4 hours before sleep weren't linked to any change in sleep, however hard they were.[^19] This is observational data from a commercial wearable company's users, so it can't prove cause, but it's a far larger and more realistic sample than the lab studies.
- **Timing also nudges your body clock.** In a study of 99 adults, moderate exercise at 7 am or between 1 and 4 pm shifted the body clock earlier, while exercise between 7 and 10 pm shifted it later.[^20] If you're a night owl trying to fall asleep earlier, morning or afternoon workouts work with you, not against you.

**Try:** Exercise whenever you can actually stick to it. That matters more than timing. Schedule hard sessions (intervals, races, heavy lifting) at least 2–4 hours before bed, and keep late-evening workouts light. If you notice you sleep worse after evening training, move it earlier.

## 7. Take a warm bath or shower 1–2 hours before bed

A meta-analysis of studies on warm baths and showers found that water around 40–42.5 °C (104–108.5 °F) was linked to better self-rated sleep quality and sleep efficiency. When taken 1–2 hours before bed, even a 10-minute soak shortened the time to fall asleep. The likely mechanism: warming the skin draws blood to the hands and feet, which helps the body shed heat and drop its core temperature, a natural signal for sleep.[^9]

**Try:** Shower or bathe about 90 minutes before bed. Too close to bedtime and you won't have cooled down yet.

## Waking up in the middle of the night

### First: some waking is normal

Waking briefly during the night is part of normal sleep. Most of us move through several sleep cycles each night and come close to waking between them, usually without remembering it. A meta-analysis of 65 sleep-lab and actigraphy studies of healthy people found that time awake after first falling asleep naturally increases with age, while sleep efficiency goes down.[^11] Waking at 3 am isn't a sign that something is broken. It becomes a problem when you can't get back to sleep, when it happens most nights, or when it leaves you tired during the day.

### What helps

**Get out of bed if you can't fall back asleep.** This is *stimulus control*, one of the core parts of CBT-I. If you've been lying awake for what feels like 15–20 minutes, get up, go to another room, do something calm in dim light, and go back to bed only when you feel sleepy. The aim is to stop your brain from learning that bed is a place for lying awake and worrying. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2021 guideline suggests stimulus control as a treatment for chronic insomnia even on its own. It recommends full multicomponent CBT-I most strongly, and advises against relying on sleep hygiene tips alone.[^12] In the trials summarized earlier, CBT-I cut time awake during the night by about 26 minutes on average.[^3]

**Don't check the clock.** In two experiments, people told to watch a clock while trying to fall asleep worried more and took longer to fall asleep than people who didn't. People with insomnia who watched the clock also overestimated how long they had been awake.[^13] Turn the clock away from the bed and leave your phone out of reach.

**Look at your evening drinks.** As described above, alcohol makes the second half of the night more fragmented, which is exactly when many middle-of-the-night wakings happen.[^6] Late caffeine also reduces total sleep.[^5]

**Keep your wake-up time fixed** after a bad night, rather than sleeping in. Sleeping in can make the next night worse. This is the same consistent-schedule principle from section 1.

### When to see a doctor

See a doctor if night waking goes along with loud snoring, gasping or choking, frequent trips to the toilet, pain, or low mood, or if it has gone on for months. Sleep apnea, a bladder or prostate problem, chronic pain, and depression can all fragment sleep, and none of them is fixed by sleep hygiene.

## Waking up with more energy

### Morning grogginess is normal, up to a point

The heavy, slow feeling right after waking has a name: *sleep inertia*. It temporarily impairs thinking and fades the longer you're awake. A review of the research found that it is worse after sleep loss, and also depends on what time of day you wake up.[^14] So some grogginess is normal. Grogginess that lasts all morning, every morning, usually points to a problem with the night before.

### What predicts a good morning

The best data here comes from a study of 833 adults, including twins, who wore activity trackers and glucose monitors and rated their alertness for several hours after waking over two weeks. How alert people felt in the morning had little to do with their genes. It was linked to four things they could change:[^15]

1. **Sleeping longer, and waking later than usual,** the night before. In practice that means more sleep, not an earlier alarm.
2. **More physical activity the previous day.**
3. **A breakfast higher in carbohydrates.**
4. **A smaller blood-sugar spike after breakfast.** Sugary breakfasts were linked to lower alertness.

This is an observational study, so it shows associations, not proof of cause. It fits with the rest of this post, though: most of a good morning is set up the day and night before.

### What about the snooze button?

Snoozing has a bad reputation, but the evidence is milder than you'd expect. In a lab study of 31 habitual snoozers, 30 minutes of snoozing cost only about 6 minutes of sleep. It didn't hurt mood or night-time sleep, and cognitive performance right after getting up was the same or slightly better than with an abrupt alarm. That may be because snoozing made people less likely to be woken from deep sleep.[^16] The study only included people who already snooze and fall back asleep easily, and it covered just one morning. In the same researchers' survey, people who snoozed tended to sleep less overall and feel drowsier in the morning. If you can't wake up without three alarms, the issue is probably too little sleep, not the button.

### A practical morning routine

- **Protect your sleep length and keep a regular wake time.** These are the two strongest levers (see section 1 and the study above).
- **Give yourself 15–30 minutes** before doing anything that needs a sharp mind or careful driving, while sleep inertia wears off.
- **Get daylight and move early**, which also helps anchor your body clock for the following night.
- **Eat a breakfast based on complex carbohydrates**, such as oats or whole grains, rather than a sugary one.
- **Be active during the day**, because it pays off the next morning.

If you sleep 7–9 hours regularly and still wake exhausted, or feel very sleepy during the day, talk to a doctor. Sleep apnea, anemia, thyroid problems, depression and some medications commonly cause unrefreshing sleep.

## What about melatonin?

A meta-analysis of 19 placebo-controlled trials with 1,683 people found that melatonin does help, but modestly: on average about 7 minutes faster to fall asleep and about 8 minutes more total sleep, with a small improvement in sleep quality. The effects didn't fade with continued use, and its side-effect profile is milder than most sleep medications.[^10]

Melatonin is more useful for shifting the timing of sleep (jet lag, a delayed body clock) than as a general sleeping pill. If you consider it, talk to a doctor or pharmacist first, particularly if you take other medication.

## A note on evidence quality

Not all of the above is equally solid:

- **Strong:** CBT-I for insomnia (multiple randomized trials, endorsed by clinical guidelines); exercise (large meta-analysis).
- **Moderate:** warm baths, melatonin (meta-analyses, but of smaller or more varied studies); the 7+ hour target (expert consensus based on observational evidence).
- **Suggestive:** caffeine timing, evening screens, clock-watching and snoozing (small, well-controlled studies); sleep regularity and morning alertness (large but observational).

## Where to start

If you only change three things:

1. Fix your wake-up time, seven days a week.
2. Move your last coffee to early afternoon.
3. If you've struggled with sleep for months, ask about CBT-I rather than pills.

And if you snore loudly, stop breathing during sleep, or are very sleepy during the day despite enough time in bed, see a doctor. Those can be signs of a sleep disorder that no amount of sleep hygiene will fix.
